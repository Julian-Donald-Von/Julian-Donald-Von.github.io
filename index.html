01Profile

Where engineering meets law Aerospace · Law · AI

I read aircraft power engineering at the School of Astronautics before moving to law school. The turn was not a flight from engineering but a transfer of its habits into legal questions: judicial discretion can be coded, the boundaries of a model’s behaviour can be measured, and the premises of a normative argument can be made explicit.

Since then, most of my work has sat on the same seam: building systems that turn legal material into something computable, and asking what it takes for the results to be usable in a legal argument. That question is harder than it looks. A number that is defensible in engineering can be worthless in law, because the law asks not only whether a result is stable but whether it can be attributed — to a person, to a design decision, to a record. Most of my projects are attempts to make that translation honest.

One line of that work is technical. I build and evaluate models that read music, speech and text, and I care about how they behave when they are small enough to run on ordinary hardware. Building a local AI radio — one that selects, introduces and comments on music in real time, without sending a single byte to a server — turned out to be a demanding test of that. It forced me to answer questions I could previously avoid: which parts of a system actually need a model, which parts merely needed a better interface, and where a delay of two hundred milliseconds stops being a performance issue and becomes a legal one.

The other line is doctrinal and empirical. I work on how private law should treat computational decisions: when a model’s output can be treated as an expression of intent, what a claimant would have to establish to challenge it, and where responsibility should rest when the causal chain runs through a system that no single person fully understands. I test those arguments against real material — hundreds of coded judgments, comparative doctrine, and the actual behaviour of deployed systems.

I hold both the Chinese Patent Agent Qualification and the National Legal Profession Qualification, and I work on the translation layer between technical fact and legal element.

02Research

Research areas Four threads

Music emotion recognition
Teaching a machine to read the emotion of a piece of music, at a cost low enough to run in real time on consumer hardware.
Efficient inference and model compression
Offline precomputation, distillation and asynchronous scheduling: fitting large models into 8 GB of VRAM and a millisecond budget.
Algorithmic accountability and legal attribution
When an automated decision causes harm, can technical transparency answer the question of who ought to be responsible?
Judicial data and empirical legal studies
Bulk retrieval, structured coding and quantitative analysis of judgments as the factual base under a normative argument.

04Work

Selected work Python · real-time audio · GPU rendering

Radio Intelligence — a local-first AI radio station

2025.08 — present

Solo project · 120+ iterations

  • Designed and built end to end: an AI DJ, LLM-driven roaming recommendation, music semantic understanding, real-time emotion visualisation, lyrics, EQ and theming.
  • FastAPI plus a WebSocket event bus, native ES modules and WebGL2, with the music-understanding layer decoupled from the visual layer.
  • A multi-clock understanding pipeline — CLAP for segment semantics, YAMNet for short-term state, Beat This! for beat events — carried through to the training side as the same three-layer shape.
  • Under an RTX 4060 with 8 GB, compute-heavy work is precomputed and indexed offline so that LLM, TTS, audio playback and WebGL rendering coexist on a single machine.
  • Led real-time audio hardening: callback cost from 14.6 ms to 0.63 ms (≈23×, 2.7% of the real-time budget), plus fixes for event-loop blocking, periodic 48/96 kHz artefacts and dropped WebSocket events.
  • Built an automated verification and regression system — 7 suites, hundreds of assertions, a zero-failure baseline — with every key fix checked by both a negative and an end-to-end test.

07Beyond

Beyond research, allegedly. The non-peer-reviewed record

Everything above this line is written the way academia expects it to be written: measured, cited, hedged in the passive voice.

This section is not. It records the parts of the practice that do not survive peer review: doctored museum reproductions, instruments built out of proportion to their questions, and annotations that would get a footnote rejected on sight.

If any of this reads as unserious, that is a misreading. The serious question underneath is the same one the rest of this page asks, asked badly on purpose: what exactly did you make, how do you know it works, and would you sign your name to the measurement.

Everything below returns to the sober register.

Selected non-peer-reviewed activities
Doctored museum reproductions with deliberately falsified provenance; period-inaccurate instrument repairs that nonetheless play in tune; marginalia in library books that were asking for it.
Known failure modes
Explaining a joke’s provenance until it stops being funny; citing oneself in dinner conversation; treating a 3D printer calibration curve as a research output.
Current research question
Whether the 3D printer can reproduce the lute before the lute’s designer notices the missing scale bar, and what error bars apply to a smile.
