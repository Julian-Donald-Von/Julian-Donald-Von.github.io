Chapter One · Pending Enquiry

The carriage for the upper station was not yet full. Its driver crouched beside the front wheel, winding a length of thin cord around a cracked mudguard.

Eno waited under the porch of the Songhan post yard, his leather bag held against him. Morning mist lay against the roofs at the end of the street. A skin of ice remained in the water channel outside; the man clearing it broke it with a wooden handle, and the pieces squeezed along with the water beneath the next paving slab. Up the slope, people were already leading mules towards the station. His father stood beside him, holding the cloth-covered bundle that contained a change of clothes, a pair of shoes and two books.

“What have you got in that bag?” his father asked.

Eno changed hands.

“Not much.”

“You’ve changed hands three times.”

His father handed him the bundle and reached for the bag. Eno would not give it up. He put it on the wooden bench himself. The heaviest things inside were two volumes of lecture notes, a book of surveying exercises and the copper flask. He had already taken one book out, closed the bag, then put it back.

The driver bit through the loose end of the cord and stood to call them aboard. Once Eno was settled, his father passed up the luggage and asked when the job in Sertin would pay.

“The paper says after a full month.”

“Keep your rent money aside first. Nobody knows you there.”

“I’ve brought it.”

His father patted his smock pocket and found a piece of bread wrapped in paper. As Eno took it, the carriage jolted. He pushed the bread beside his leg to free a hand for the seat in front. His father stepped back twice. The carriage hid half of him; then it turned the corner, and both the man and the porch were gone.

Songhan lay to the north, in the Frostgate Marquisate, with hillside fields and woodland outside the town. People taking the country roads east or west often changed carriages at the post yard. Eno’s father kept the accounts at the main postal counter. When he put down his pen in the afternoon, someone would often stop him to ask which station a letter had reached. Sometimes the person asking could not even give the full address. His father would open the ledger and find the entries, one line at a time. As a child, Eno had done his writing behind the counter. When nobody was at home, that was where he waited.

He knew the order of the shop signs in town with his eyes shut. At the north end of Post Street stood a few maples, their spring buds not yet fully open. The road doubling back to the upper station had already been resurfaced. At the station, people and pack animals separated, and someone took charge of the mailbags. Eno sat in the waiting shelter with the bag on his lap and ate half the bread.

Southbound, the place names changed at every stop, though much of the farmland and the bone slopes outside looked alike. Sometimes he fell asleep. When he woke, he checked the bag first, then leaned towards the glass to find the station name. Several sets of passengers came and went. A man in a woollen waistcoat lifted the basket from between his feet so Eno could stretch his legs, then got off with it two stops later. Eno could not remember where he had boarded. He remembered only a pinch of hay that had leaked through the bottom of the basket and caught between two seat boards.

At Shumen he bought a flaskful of hot water and made coffee in the waiting shelter. The paper packet went back into the inner side of his bag. His certificate was held between two pieces of stiff card; he had kept it dry all through buying the ticket. He had copied his new address into a different little notebook. He read the line beneath “Works Street” twice before following the other passengers to find the connecting train for Sertin.

He did not sleep on this last stretch. Houses grew more numerous. Small workshops stood behind vegetable plots, and every so often a freight siding branched across the view. Near the city the train slowed. A row of goods sheds with their doors open slid past the window. A man in one of them raised his hand and called something to a passenger he knew. An answer came from another window. Before Eno could make it out, the train had left the shed behind.

When he got off, the bag handle had left a red mark across his palm. Outside the station he put it down and opened his hand. Luggage carts crossed the rail joints, their wheels sounding one after another. White steam billowed out of the shed and dispersed beneath the roofs around the square.

The building opposite had four storeys. A gallery ran outside its second-floor windows. People were walking along it when, suddenly, a carriage passed above their heads.

Eno looked up. It was on a short bridge farther away. One end of the bridge disappeared behind a building; when the carriage emerged again, it was in front of another row of windows. Morning light caught a higher railing. Behind it grew trees, with a few chairs beneath them. He stepped back to see the whole of it and knocked his bag against somebody’s knee.

“Coming through.”

He lifted it at once and moved to the side. Most of the square was still in shade. A narrow strip at the top of the clock face shone.

The stop board listed Sunterrace, Arcade Market and Works Street. A smaller board nailed beneath it read: CHANGE FOR SUNTERRACE.

Eno found Works Street, memorised the fare beside it and moved the bag to his other hand. He did not take the tram.

The inn on North Station Street was still gathering its sheets. A wooden sign at the entrance said ONE TRAIN LATER. Smaller lettering to one side listed beds, luggage storage and hot water; laundry cost extra. A woman behind the counter was unhooking a key from a string of wooden tags.

“How many nights?”

“Three to start with.”

“A bed?”

Eno glanced into the passage. Two men were bringing a large trunk down the narrow stairs. The lower one had his face pressed against its corner. He let out a breath.

“How much for a little room?”

The woman named the price.

He put back a small coin, took out one of a larger denomination, then counted out a few more to make up the difference.

“Three nights to start with,” he said.

“This one, then.” The woman untied a wooden tag from the key. “I’m Meta. Up the stairs, on the right. Basin under the bed. Fetch your water from the landing. No open flames at night.”

The room had a bed and a chair. The back of the chair touched the edge of the table; to sit down, he had to pull it out at an angle. Outside the window was the back wall of the house opposite. A line ran between two windows, and a wet shirt hung towards his side. Water dripped from the tip of a sleeve onto sheet metal below.

He took out his certificate, laid it flat on the table and undid the strap at the side of the bag.

The small copper flask was rather flat, just right for lying against the side of the bag. Its lower half was dark; the places he held were bright. A ring of white tin edged the mouth. Eno wiped the moisture from it with his thumb, raised the flask and took a mouthful. He moved the coffee from his left cheek to his right. His throat shifted, and the bitterness at the back of his tongue slowly receded.

He had drunk from the batch made at Shumen only twice on the journey. He took out the paper packet too, untied its string and pinched up a corner to look inside before tying it again. The roaster’s address was printed on the paper, the same shop on Post Street in Songhan. Usually the owner put his two measures into one packet. This time he had asked for them separately, so they would be less likely to get damp on the road.

Eno tightened the flask cap, changed his coat and went downstairs to ask the way.

At “General Survey Office”, Meta’s cleaning cloth stopped.

“Seventh Branch? Works Street. When you go out, don’t take that bridge. Go underneath.”

She pointed it out again.

Works Street was full of people holding papers.

Map tubes stood beside the benches. Open sheets were spread across knees. A man held up a newly written document in a draught to dry it, his other hand holding a child. The child was pulling him towards an instrument shop window. Several brass compasses hung behind the glass, all their needles pointing the same way.

Eno looked too as he passed.

First he showed his certificate and appointment paper at the branch, then was sent to the Public Service Hall. The two buildings were a little way apart on the same street. Both had stone steps and windows divided into small panes. Only when he saw a board saying OCCUPATIONAL SERVICES did he know he had come to the right place this time.

The person behind the window opened his certificate.

“Local address.”

“One Train Later Inn, North Station Street.”

“Room number.”

Eno gave it.

“The term of employment is as stated here. The responsible officer has signed. You sign below.”

He removed his pen cap. The box was narrow. At the final stroke he drew his hand in a little, keeping clear of the printed line.

The clerk aligned the certificate with the paper, stamped them and kept one sheet behind the window.

“Keep this safe. Your certificate too. Next.”

Back at the branch, the man assigning work was looking for a missing clip. He moved two ledgers. The clip was under the desk. He nudged it out with his shoe and bent to pick it up.

“North Trestle Ward. Resurvey of a branch drain. Bring back what’s actually there. Don’t rub things off the map yourself. Leave the verification box empty.”

He gave Eno a folded map and a few blank forms, then pulled a thin book from a shelf.

“Field book. Date it every day. Return borrowed instruments when they’re due. Record any knocks too. Don’t wait until the next shift finds something wrong, then come asking who did it.”

“I hold an entry-level certificate for employed surveying,” Eno said.

The man looked up at him.

“I saw. You measure, you record, you sign for the measurements. Someone else signs the verification.”

Eno took his hand away from the certificate pocket.

The surveyor’s level came in a wooden case lined at the opening with dark green velvet. The telescope and base had pressed their shapes into the pile. One patch had worn thin, showing the pale threads underneath. The three levelling screws offered much the same resistance; the eyepiece ring was a little stiff, then eased after a short turn. Supporting the instrument inside its case, he tried it twice without forcing it.

An issue slip lay beside it, already bearing an instrument number. He checked each digit against the little brass plate inside the case before signing his name.

“Benna. Go with him.”

A man by the door answered.

Eno had thought he was there to mend the door. He was in his fifties, with his cap on his knee. The pockets of his grey-brown smock sagged low, half a length of hemp cord sticking out. He stood up, fetched a tea bowl from the windowsill and drank what remained.

“Eno?”

“Yes.”

“Give me the case.”

“I’ll carry it.”

Benna went to get the levelling staff instead. Before they left, he held out a very small folded paper.

“When we get back each day, sign for the hours on here. Overtime only if we’ve worked it. If we haven’t, don’t put it down.”

Eno unfolded it.

“It has to be what we actually worked.”

“That’s what I’m asking you for,” Benna said.

In North Trestle Ward the road divided into three before a short bridge.

The map showed only two.

Eno stopped and turned the folded sheet halfway round. Benna had already taken the middle way. After a few paces he noticed Eno was not following and stood the staff beside his foot.

“This way.”

“Where does it go?”

“Where we’re going.”

“I meant the street name.”

“Ask the baker, he says behind the oven. Ask the people over there, it’s the lower end of Upper Trestle.” Benna pointed ahead. “Farther in, it’s Back Street.”

Eno wrote “Back Street” in the margin and added a small question mark.

Benna saw it and said nothing.

The houses resembled those on Works Street: pale walls, tall windows, a fine moulding under the eaves. But much had been added underneath. Wooden stairs joined brick piers, coal baskets were stuffed beneath the stairs, and another layer of boards covered an old platform. A door leading upstairs opened beside the bridge. Someone inside passed out a washed basin; the person outside took it and set it behind them on the railing.

A freight wagon was stuck at the corner. Its driver had got down and was backing it by the shafts while two porters steadied the high boxes. Benna waited at the side, quite settled in his waiting. Eno drew the wooden case a little closer against his chest.

Above and to one side he noticed a drainpipe whose bend had been patched with two differently coloured sheets of metal. A sloping slab beneath its mouth sent the water into the channel along the wall. A narrow board was missing from the channel cover. Someone had filled the gap with broken brick, now worn into dark hollows by passing feet.

“Start here?” he asked.

“First find a point we can identify. Set up here and that wagon’ll take you with it when it backs up.”

On the first day they began from the benchmark outside an old maintenance door.

Benna knew the painted number beside it and which man inside held the key. While they waited for him, he sat on the stone edge and smoked. Eno spread the map on the case lid but remained standing, studying the key, then the old marks on the wall.

It was a very tidy base map. Main drains, branch drains and inspection openings each had their own lines. An earlier freight-yard boundary was also printed over this part of North Trestle Ward. A stretch of the road they had just walked fell inside it. The stairs and doors built along that stretch were not shown.

An old map, Eno thought.

The next day he took his new sketch to the old-map archive and requested the sheet on which the base map had been founded.

It was not on the original either.

The archivist found a later supplementary sheet. A shed had been added, but the passage beside it was still blank. By then the wooden sign for the midday closure had been turned round. The archivist pushed the borrowing register towards him and asked him to return the maps.

“But people live there now.”

“The housing registers are in the west wing. You asked for a drainage map.”

Eno signed the return and kept the map numbers. Outside, Benna was waiting under the eaves with two hot potatoes wrapped in paper.

“You really can keep looking till they shut.”

He offered one.

Eno did not take it at once. First he reached for his money. Benna gave the price and waited for him to find the change before putting the potato in his hand.

“Going back tomorrow?”

“Yes. There’s a stretch farther in that hasn’t been tied in yet.”

Benna opened the top of his own paper and blew on it.

On the third morning Eno set up the instrument in the lane without a street sign.

First there was the sound of knife sharpening. Someone drew a kitchen knife twice across a stone in a doorway, splashed it with water and went on. Farther in, a small window had been propped open. The smell of onions in a pan came out beneath the sill, mingling with wet timber and the sour smell of the drain.

Benna held the staff at the other end. He tucked his cigarette behind his ear, wiped his palm across a reasonably clean patch beside the wall and planted his feet.

Eno bent to turn the eyepiece.

Inside the round field of view, plaster and a door frame moved aside. The black and white divisions on the staff came clear. Very fine hairs crossed at the centre, the horizontal one resting on the lower edge of a division. He took his eye away to check the tubular spirit level. Two etched lines enclosed a long, narrow bubble. With a thumb and forefinger on each of two screws, he turned them inwards together. The bubble moved; he turned them back. One tripod foot stood beside the joint between two paving slabs. He pressed the point down more firmly and waited.

The divisions in the telescope stopped moving.

“Staff a little farther out.”

Benna shifted it.

“Too far. Back half a hand.”

A wooden wheel sounded behind Eno. He did not look up. He wrote down the reading first and rested his pen on the next line.

“Move that a bit.”

The woman with the handcart stood behind the tripod. Two baskets were on her cart: one of root vegetables still carrying soil, the other packed with bunches of greens. Damp sacking covered one corner. She wore a faded blue smock with its sleeves rolled to the elbows. Bits of leaf clung to the backs of her hands; the places that gripped the handles had been rubbed clean.

“Just a moment. Let me finish this section.”

“Fold one of those legs in and let me through.”

Eno glanced back. The wooden wheel would clear the tripod foot, but the basket would not. The one on the right stuck out beyond the cart.

“If I move that foot, I have to level it again. I won’t be long.”

“I’ve got to get to market.”

“Five minutes.”

The woman lowered the handles. The cart’s support struck the stone with a thock. She rubbed her forehead with the heel of her hand and looked towards the mouth of the lane.

Eno bent to the eyepiece again. Benna had taken the cigarette from behind his ear. He held it unlit.

The two readings at this setup differed by more than Eno had expected. The point carried forward from the day before ought to be right. He turned back through his records, then looked at the staff again. The woman pressed the protruding leaves into the basket. A drop fell from the sacking.

“Finished?”

“One more reading.”

“You said five minutes.”

Eno took out his watch and looked.

“It’s only just over five minutes.”

“Eno, let her through first,” Benna called from the far end.

“I haven’t finished this setup.”

Benna rested the staff against his shoulder.

“I’ll put it back for you.”

“Not the staff. If I take this down, the whole setup has to be done again.”

The woman straightened, both hands still on the cart handles.

“I got these vegetables ready first thing, came round this way, and by the time you’re done measuring, will they still be keeping me a place?”

“Can’t they keep your pitch for you a little while?”

She was tightening a loose cuff back above her elbow. Her hand stopped. She pulled the cart backwards and tried to turn it. Someone else had entered the lane behind her with a wooden bucket full of things. He stood against the wall to see how they would make room.

“Can you go round the outside?” Eno asked.

The woman looked at him.

Benna bent his head and rubbed the mud off the foot of the staff.

“That cartful is her day’s living. Let her through. If you need to measure it again, I’ll stay and do it with you.”

“Two more minutes. Nearly done.”

Having said it, he hurried back to the eyepiece. The horizontal hair cut exactly between a black division and a white one. He wrote the reading, turned a page forwards and moved his pen between two figures. The wind lifted a corner of the paper; he pinned it with his wrist. The tripod point he had never quite managed to press home shifted beside the joint in the paving.

He stopped to look at the bubble again.

Behind him, the bottom of a basket scraped against the wall.

The woman pulled back the damp sacking, pushed the overhanging greens in and removed a crosspiece from the edge of the basket. She turned the front of the cart sideways, braced its rim with her knee and lifted with both hands. The man with the bucket put it down and reached to steady the outer basket.

“Careful,” Eno said.

He shielded the telescope with one hand and held down the case lid with the other. The basket swept towards the copper flask beside the case. He let go of the lid and caught the flask in his hand.

A wooden wheel passed suspended against a tripod leg.

Benna had come over to support the front of the cart. The three of them squeezed it sideways through the narrow stretch. The woman’s sleeve brushed the wet wall and came away with a black patch. A loose bunch of greens opened. Several plants fell beside Eno’s shoe, fine white rootlets still hanging from them.

The cart landed with another thock.

The woman pushed the crosspiece back into place and took up the handles. She did not bend to pick anything up. Two paces on, she swore.

Not loudly.

The other man picked up his bucket and passed between Eno and the wall. Its rim knocked his clipboard. Eno drew the board in and waited until the footsteps had gone before releasing his grip on the flask.

Benna went back to hold the staff.

The bubble was out. Eno slowly turned the levelling screws back, took a reading, then took it again. They matched. He entered it in the book and checked the earlier figures. At last they agreed.

Beside the drain mouth, a length of stone channel made a small turn before entering the old inspection channel. They used a tape to measure its actual length separately: four metres twenty. Benna held the end, his cigarette behind his ear again. Eno pressed his fingernail exactly against the graduation.

Four point two. He wrote it down and placed a dot beneath the word “metres”.

When he packed the tripod away, the greens beside his shoe had been trodden on. Stalks were broken, moisture spreading from the crushed parts. He picked up one plant. Soil from its roots worked between his fingers. He looked towards the end of the lane. The woman and her cart were long gone.

“Leave it on the edge of the drain,” Benna said.

Eno put it there.

Benna tied up the staff, took the cigarette from behind his ear and crouched by the low wall across the street to light it.

“You should have let her through.”

“She got through in the end, didn’t she?”

“Did you let her?”

Eno closed the wooden case. The catch missed. He pressed down the lid and tried it again.

“I told her I wouldn’t be long. If she’d waited a little, I’d have finished.”

“She couldn’t afford to wait.”

“I have my work to do too.”

Benna drew on his cigarette.

Somewhere farther off, a tram bell sounded twice. He took the cigarette away from his mouth and steadied the staff against the wall while the tram passed.

Eno put the records in his bag. A small piece of leaf was caught beneath the buckle. He picked it off. It stuck to his finger, and he had to shake his hand twice before it fell.

“There’s another section farther in,” he said.

Benna stood.

Deep in the lane was an inspection opening, half its cover beneath a house’s eaves.

Eno crouched and knocked with the backs of his fingers. Someone answered from inside. A woman opened the door, saw the staff, then their smocks, and called into the room, “They’ve come about the drain.”

Benna said they were only measuring today, not taking anything apart.

“Just as well.” The woman hooked the door open. “If you are taking it up, tell us beforehand. The stove’ll have to move.”

She pushed a basin behind the door with her foot to give them room. Inside, a child was looking for shoes beneath the table. One was found. The other remained in the child’s hand, never put on.

An old number remained on the corner of the cover. Eno wiped it with a cloth and made out two digits. The map showed an opening in roughly the same place, but facing the wrong way. The branch drain should have come in from a patch of unbuilt ground.

The family’s doorway now opened onto that ground.

“How long have you lived here?” Eno asked.

“I moved in seven years ago.”

“Has the drain always been here?”

“It was here when I came.”

She looked at his open map and pointed towards the room.

“Is that supposed to be my house?”

Eno folded the paper back a little to show the whole freight-yard boundary.

“This map doesn’t mark it separately.”

“They manage to mark the rent separately every month.”

Benna gave a laugh and did not add anything.

The woman went inside for a receipt, thinking that was what Eno wanted to inspect. Its edges were furry. A narrow strip of paper repaired a fold, and the sum was written in the middle. Eno took it and saw the payee’s name and a house number.

The number was not on his form.

“I’m not checking your rent.” He handed it back. “I need to add this part.”

“And once it’s added, you can mend it?”

Eno glanced at Benna.

“It has to go back for verification first.”

“Well, you get on with measuring.”

She put the receipt in her apron pocket and went to hurry the child into its shoes. Eno drew the position of the door on his sketch and added two neighbours. He followed the drain all the way to the other end of the lane. People kept coming from there too. Baskets stood against the houses; when a cart approached, a hand reached out through a doorway to drag the outermost one inside. Eno stepped out of the way and turned the map in his hands again.

At midday they rested by the main street.

Benna took his food from his bag and sat at one end of an unused bench. Eno bought bread, spread the clean half of its wrapping on his knee and opened his field book to work the sketch into the forms.

He made the surveyed lines distinct from the old map lines, leaving the freight-yard boundary untouched. The added doors, drains and junctions had another kind of line. In the margin he noted the number of the old map used. On the third page he stopped at the seventh box.

Anchor-point number.

He had measured a drain. The houses along it had brick footings and wooden posts. In some places bone showed; elsewhere it was buried beneath brick and earth. Which post rested on which approved anchor point was not something he had checked today.

“Benna, is there a number here?”

Benna finished his mouthful before leaning over.

“Didn’t see one outside.”

“Shall we ask?”

“Ask who? The woman renting that house, or the rope seller up ahead?”

Eno put a pencil mark in the box to remind himself to ask at the office. When Benna had finished eating, he unfolded the overtime slip and tapped it with a knuckle.

“Yesterday’s half-hour.”

Eno checked the times in his own book and signed. Benna folded the paper, put it into the little pocket inside his smock, patted it and picked up the staff.

That afternoon the branch’s receiving clerk turned to the third page.

“Box seven isn’t filled in.”

“I couldn’t find an anchor-point number for that section. Today was a branch-drain resurvey.”

“You have to write something even if you couldn’t find it. It can’t be blank. A blank doesn’t tell me whether you missed it or haven’t checked.”

Eno took out his pen.

“Then ‘None’?”

“If there isn’t one, put ‘None’. Check the section register at the west wing. If there’s a number, add it. You’ve put in a passage, so this sheet needs to go over there.”

He returned the form with a referral slip clipped to it. Eno held the paper down with his ruler and wrote “None”. He waited until the ink was dry before folding the third page back into place.

There were more people in the west wing of the Public Service Hall than there had been that morning. A petition writer waited at the door with three sets of papers, going in every so often to ask. Eno stood behind him with the map tube. When his turn came, the clerk’s first question was whether he had a referral slip.

He passed it through.

The clerk followed the old boundary on the map, then turned to the form.

“You can’t put ‘None’ here.”

“The branch told me to.”

“Have you checked the register, or did you just not find it in the street?”

“I didn’t find it on site.”

“Then put ‘Pending Enquiry’. ‘None’ means you checked and there isn’t one.”

Eno took out his pen. His thumb rested against the cap, but he did not pull it off.

“There are houses here already. Residents with rent receipts. And a road you can walk all the way through.”

“Residents are residents. A road is a road. You’ve drawn the lines in, but who’s going to acknowledge responsibility for this piece of ground? Does the boundary change with them?”

“I haven’t changed the boundary. The old line is here.”

He pushed the map forwards and touched the retained line with a fingernail. The clerk flattened the paper, checked the map number in the margin first, then took an index from the shelf. After turning a few pages, the clerk pressed a palm against the spine.

The petition writer outside moved half a step in.

“Mine only needs—”

“Wait,” the clerk said.

The pen shaft travelled to the end of a line of small print and back to the beginning. The clerk bent closer, turned a page and folded the previous one back between two fingers. Eno’s hand was still resting on the map’s edge. Between them the paper had pulled taut. He let go and allowed it to lie flat.

At last the clerk closed the index.

“This stretch isn’t entered in the public branch-drain register. Put ‘Pending Enquiry’ for now. Record what you measured on site. The status of the houses and the right of passage need a separate check.”

“Who checks them?”

“It gets assigned once it’s taken in.”

Eno returned to the branch for correction paper. “None” was already written on the third page. To change it to “Pending Enquiry”, he had to sign beside the alteration. The earlier ink could not be blacked out. Following the rule, he drew one fine line through it, leaving the old word legible.

He laid his pen down carefully and drank some coffee. It was no longer warm. Left cheek, right cheek, swallow. The bottom of the flask tapped lightly against his palm.

When he returned to the Public Service Hall, a TEMPORARILY AWAY sign hung at the west-wing window. Someone beside it said referred papers now went to Receiving Point Three in the central court. He walked round the courtyard. A man came out carrying an empty bowl; under the eaves two pigeons disputed a fallen crust.

The clerk at Point Three was an older woman with spectacles perched on her nose. She read “Pending Enquiry” and reached towards the slip clipped to the form.

“Explanatory note.”

“It’s in the map margin.”

“Separate attachment. Old map number, extent of the survey, which stretch is missing from the register. Make it clear. If I take only these sheets, the verifier will have to call you back to ask.”

Eno found a table under the covered walkway.

Ink marked one corner, with a layer of loose dust on top. He dragged the chair aside and cleared enough space for his paper. First he listed the old map number, then described the inspection opening, the position of the household’s door and the course of the drain. At the end he added a paragraph, setting “Records existing conditions only; makes no change to rights or title” on a line of its own.

Benna was waiting outside the courtyard gate. He came to ask once how much longer it would be. Eno said nearly done. When Eno finished and passed the sheet into Point Three, the older clerk covered its lower half with her hand.

“Where do the verification remarks go?”

Eno looked at her hand.

“Isn’t the back blank?”

“This kind is filed on one side only. Once it’s folded, the back doesn’t go into the register.”

She placed a blank specimen beside it. There were ruled lines at the top and more below, separated by the faintly printed word VERIFICATION. Eno’s paper had been covering that word.

“Keep the account shorter. What you measured, where it differs. Leave the lower half free.”

He took the sheet back.

Benna was no longer outside the courtyard gate. The gatekeeper said the man with the long staff had returned to Maintenance. His things were in the lodge; this slip would let Eno collect them. Eno took it. On the back, Benna had written: Don’t forget to return the case.

At the same table Eno sat down again. He removed the descriptions of the houses one by one, leaving the positions of the doors on the map. He crossed out a few repeated sentences and rewrote the note in half a page.

On the final line he slowed down to fit the remaining words inside the rule. The top of the half-page was reasonably open; towards the bottom the writing grew closer. He read it from beginning to end, folded the full sheet into his bag and picked up the one with half a page left blank.

The clerk straightened the papers, counted them and clipped them together.

“All right. Leave it.”

“When will it be approved?”

“It still has to be sent round.”

“Roughly how many days?”

She looked at another pile beside her.

“Can’t say. Wait for notice. Keep the receipt. Bring it if you add anything.”

Eno waited until she had finished writing the date clearly, then drew the receipt from beneath the window. The map stayed inside, with his new explanatory note on top. Beneath the half-page of writing, several lines were empty.

At the courtyard gate he looked back once.

A man carrying a parcel of papers hurried up and asked if this was where an omitted death registration could be added. The gatekeeper pointed the other way. The man turned at once. A strip of black cloth slipped from beneath the parcel.

Eno picked up the instruments and left.

When he reached the inn, Meta was counting sheets by the counter for the laundry.

“Staying on?”

Eno stopped.

“Yes.”

“Tomorrow morning’s fine for the money. Someone asked about the room, so I thought I’d tell you.”

He took out the money, counted it and laid it on the corner of the counter. Meta freed one hand from the sheets to collect it and turned the wooden tag over.

The shirt outside his window had gone. A pair of thick trousers hung on the line instead. Eno pulled the chair out at an angle, sat down and first slipped the receipt into his book. The instrument issue slip went to one side. He would need the equipment again tomorrow and had to extend the borrowing dates.

Two drops of coffee came out of the copper flask.

He lowered its mouth a little farther and shook it. Nothing.

He had finished it earlier than usual. He had drunk at the branch, while waiting in the west wing and again before writing the note. On the table, the slanting light across the flask’s shoulder showed a very small dent. He pressed his thumb against it. The empty flask felt oddly light.

Someone was carrying hot water along the corridor. He went out with his basin. Back in the room, he poured water through the cloth filter bag left from that morning a second time. It still gave colour, but little smell. He poured it into the flask, left the cap beside it and bent to check the fair-copy duplicate he had kept.

Third page.

He turned the field book back to the measured entry, held it with his forefinger and looked at the duplicate.

Four point seven.

Eno shifted in the chair and placed the two sheets side by side.

The field book said four point two. There was still the dot he had put under “metres”. On the duplicate, the horizontal stroke of the seven lay quite flat; the stroke below was clear too. The ink was thoroughly dry.

The submitted fair copy had been copied from this duplicate, line by line.

He checked the entries on either side as well. The one above was right. The one below was right. Only this one.

From downstairs came the sound of sheets being dragged. Someone laughed. Meta told him not to let the clean end touch the floor. Eno held his pen and waited for the footsteps to pass on the stairs before drawing one line through four point seven. Beside it he wrote four point two, signed and dated the change.

The alteration stayed inside the box. Both the old figure and the new one could be read.

He capped his pen and looked at the line. After a while he uncapped it again, wrote the page number and item requiring correction on another sheet and slipped it in front of the receipt.

The copper flask had gone cold. He screwed on its cap without drinking again.

The next day he went to the branch first to report the copying error.

The receiving clerk asked him to leave one copy of the correction and take another, with his receipt, to the Public Service Hall to be attached to the original. The original had already been sent on. The person at the window noted where it had gone, placed the correction in the forwarding register and issued another receipt.

“Wait for verification. Don’t submit a whole new set yourself.”

Eno clipped the two receipts together.

A row of shovels leaned beside the Maintenance doorway. He waited there a little, then went in to ask for Benna. Someone opened the duty book and pointed to a newly added entry.

“Transferred to another section. You’re on your own today.”

“When does he come back?”

The man was already looking for the next slip and did not raise his head.

“Not scheduled yet.”

In North Trestle Ward the man behind the maintenance door recognised him and unlocked it. Eno set up in a wider place and began with the short stretches he could manage alone, bracing the staff securely, then walking back to read it. Where someone would have to hold it, he made a note and left that part until help could be assigned.

A cart sounded in the narrow lane again. He stopped, carried the tripod against the wall and let it pass, then came back to level the instrument afresh. It took longer than he had expected.

As he crouched beside the drain to record a figure, someone carrying a basket stepped over his head. He ducked, dragging a short pencil mark across the paper. The person said “Coming through” and was already gone.

He marked the stray stroke out clearly, finished the reading and took out the copper flask.

Yesterday’s second brewing was very cold, with a stale bitterness. He drank once, swallowing straight down without moving it from cheek to cheek. The cap clicked softly as he tightened it all the way. He loosened it a little, so it would open next time.

The low wall across the street was empty. A few scraps of cigarette paper lay at its foot.

Eno braced a hand on his knee to stand. The knee cracked. He tucked the clipboard under his arm and went to move the staff first.

The next section lay round the corner.
