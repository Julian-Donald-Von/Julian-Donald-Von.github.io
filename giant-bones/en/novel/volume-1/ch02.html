Chapter Two · Chickens and Soup

At the turn of the second-floor stairs, Taya’s toe struck the upturned iron basin.

Its rim scraped against the stone step. She steadied herself on the wall and pushed it farther in to make room for her foot. Someone had brought it there when the roof leaked last week. The leak had stopped. The basin remained, pinning down a white ring that had dried completely.

Downstairs, half the Third Nail’s shutter boards had been taken down.

Mata was bending to reach the lowest iron hook. The boards leaned one by one against the wall, the last muddy along its edge. At the footsteps he straightened, the hook still hanging from his hand.

“Not going to North Trestle Way today?”

“Bridge Market.”

“Shops are dear there.”

“I know.”

He nodded, dropped the hook into the box behind the door and asked no more. The little clock inside the shop had just passed half past eight. As its minute hand moved, something in the case made a very faint scraping sound.

A water carrier came into the lane. Boards covered his two buckets, but water still splashed from their edges, leaving a broken trail. He changed shoulders at the foot of the slope before starting up.

The man from the lamp-repair shop next door put his head out.

“Haven’t they got a pipe up there?”

“The pipe’s having a day off,” the water carrier said.

He could not. The pole settled on his shoulder again, and he went up the slope. Mata moved the remaining shutter boards. Taya passed his shop and turned into Reflected Light Lane.

A little ring of people stood below the mirror platform. A replacement diagonal brace had been painted against rust, the surface still shiny. Two mirror workers stood on the iron frame, one holding the handwheel, the other craning to watch where the light fell in the lane.

“Right. A bit more.”

The handwheel turned halfway, with a fine sound beneath the frame. A patch brightened on the opposite wall, passing the sill and its pot of greenery, crossing the lintel and sliding onto the paving. Its edges trembled. The joints between the stones inside it stood out clearly, even the wet moss showing green.

The breakfast seller pushed his stove half a step towards it. A woman carrying a child shifted too, turning him to let that little light fall on his face.

Tina of Backstitch Tailors was late coming out. She and her apprentice carried the worktable by its ends. By the time they had set it firmly outside, the light had left its corner.

“Stop, stop!”

The man above was still turning.

“I said stop!”

“It isn’t adjusted yet!”

“Then get it adjusted!”

The breakfast seller laughed. Tina looked up at him. He hurried to steady the kettle on his stove and closed his mouth. The apprentice still held the table edge, waiting to be told where to move it.

Taya passed outside the ring of people. The light swept across the pale worn leather at her toe, then picked out a diagonal seam along the side of her right shoe. She drew her foot back to make room for Tina. The mirror tipped, and sunlight fell into the flower seller’s bucket. The water shone. Shadows of a few white flowers rocked on its bottom.

She did not stay to wait for it to settle.

On Lakeside Street more sky showed between the roofs. The hillside railway shelter stood beside the road, one corner sagging, last night’s water not yet fully drained away. There were three benches underneath. The middle one had a broken cross rail twisted together with wire. People waiting for the train sat at the two ends, preferring to be crowded.

The queue already reached the stall selling rope fittings. Taya joined it and felt her inner pocket. Two men carrying tool bags were talking about North Trestle Way. One said freight carts had been stopped again yesterday. The other pushed back his cap: stopped or not, it cost money. Take the outer route over those two slopes and the hauliers charged extra too.

Farther ahead, a woman held a sleeping child. He had crushed the heels of his own shoes. She supported his legs and tried to pull a shoe up. Failing, she tucked the exposed foot into the edge of her skirt.

At the head of the queue an old woman stood guard over a chicken crate.

It lay crosswise beside her feet, nailed together from boards taken out of something else and divided into two compartments. Grey cloth covered the top, with narrow gaps near the handle. When a chicken moved, the bottom board gave a small sound. Two upright tail feathers poked from under one corner of the cloth.

The train came down from Bonecross Valley. After the whistle, four short carriages followed the slope into the station. When the wheels stopped, coal dust hung before the open doors. The people getting off filled the platform and forced the queue backwards. A basket of vegetables came through a window first. By the time its owner emerged, someone had moved it beside a bench. He searched the crowd until the person who had caught it jerked a chin towards it.

With his ticket book under his arm, the conductor checked what people were bringing aboard. He hefted a sack first, charged its owner for a freight ticket and tucked the corner beneath the cord at its neck. Reaching the old woman, he stopped.

“No live chickens on the train.”

The old woman looked down at the crate lid as though she had not heard.

“Live poultry,” he said again. “You can’t bring that.”

Taya craned past the two people between them.

“Can’t come on, or can’t come on for free?”

The conductor turned.

“Nothing live comes on.”

“Which of us in this queue isn’t live?”

One of the workers snorted. The conductor shut his ticket book and looked at Taya.

“I mean livestock.”

“What about fish? I’ve seen people bring fish.”

“Dead fish. In freight boxes.”

“Then lend us a knife—”

“Young lady.” The old woman looked up at last. “These two still have eggs to lay.”

“I wasn’t really going to kill them.”

Taya squeezed forwards. The man ahead turned half a shoulder to let her through, but a buckle on his tool bag still caught her sleeve. She freed it, her arm already reaching over the crate.

“Look at the box. Nailed underneath, boards on all four sides, a lid on top. They can’t run out or fly out. If you’re afraid they’ll touch somebody, I’ll tie another rope round it for her.”

“Ten ropes wouldn’t make a difference.”

“Someone brought ducks last time.”

“What day? Who?”

She could not say. All she remembered was someone selling those ducks in Bridge Market. The conductor waited for her to finish and tapped a finger on the hard cover of his ticket book.

“Then they weren’t caught.”

“There we are. You’ve caught them today, so let’s talk about it.”

“Those at the back, move up. People aboard first.”

He turned sideways to let them pass. The old woman stayed beside the crate. Nobody behind her moved either. Only the woman with the child tightened her hold and looked towards the carriage door.

“She’s brought them all the way here,” Taya said. “Where do you expect her to put them?”

“Take them home.”

“And does she travel or not?”

“She can. The chickens can’t.”

“Who’ll watch them when she’s gone? You?”

“I’ve got a whole trainful of people to watch.”

Someone by the front of the train called for them to board. The conductor answered, raised his ticket book and did not manage to open it. The old woman crouched to feel the knot in the cord. Inside, a chicken pecked the boards, pushed its head against the cloth and withdrew when the cloth would not move.

“Shouldn’t have lifted the cover,” someone behind them said.

“They made such a noise on the way,” the old woman said. “I thought they were stifling.”

“Well, they aren’t stifling now.”

She looked up. The speaker shifted towards the carriage door and avoided her eyes.

Taya pointed to the sack checked earlier and asked whether freight could travel with an extra ticket. The conductor said it could. She pointed to the remaining space inside the carriage.

“A seated ticket gets you a seat, doesn’t it?”

“Of course.”

“What if she buys two for the chickens?”

The conductor stared at her.

“Chickens aren’t people.”

“I know. If they were, you’d have sold those tickets already.”

The old woman replaced the cloth, keeping one hand on the crate. The train guard beckoned again. People waiting at the doors began to call out. The conductor pressed his lips together, looked into the carriage, opened his ticket book, shut it again and finally bent towards the old woman.

“That crate stays shut all the way. If they dirty the seats, you pay. One ticket for you, one for each bird. Full-fare seated tickets.”

“So they can come on?” Taya asked.

He ignored her and kept looking at the old woman.

“Three. Buy them and you all go. Otherwise only you can board. If anyone asks at the other end, you explain it yourself.”

The old woman undid the blue handkerchief inside her jacket. Its four corners were knotted tightly. She picked at them with her nails, then spread it on her knees. Small-denomination notes were rolled tight inside, with a one-yuan note among them and fen coins gathered at the bottom. She separated what she needed to pay, then counted the remainder again.

Taya rested a hand on the crate handle, ready to help lift it.

“I’ll buy them,” the old woman said.

The conductor took the money, gave change, entered the train and seat numbers and tore off three tickets. The old woman kept one in her hand. She slipped the other two under raised splinters in the lid, one for each compartment.

“Don’t let them make a noise,” he told her.

“Can’t promise that.”

She lifted the crate, then paused as she passed Taya.

“You’re in no hurry, then.”

“I am.”

“Then why stick your nose in?”

The handle left Taya’s hand. The old woman was already heading for the door. Taya stayed where she was for a moment. The person behind touched her arm; only then did she turn and step over to the wooden ticket booth.

One ticket, one jiao. She handed over the money, counted the jiao notes in her change and put them in her pocket.

At the door the conductor bent to inspect tickets. The old woman gave him hers and was about to step aboard when he stopped her.

“The other two.”

“On the crate.”

He bent down, moved the cloth with his fingers and reached for the tickets beneath the splinters. A chicken thrust its beak through a gap and pecked the base of his finger.

“Ow!”

He jerked his hand away. The crate rocked, both chickens cried out and everyone inside the carriage turned. The old woman hurried to support the bottom and patted the lid.

“I said no noise!” The conductor looked at his reddening hand.

“It doesn’t understand you.”

Taya put her face round from behind. He glared, and she closed her mouth and got her own ticket ready.

The next time he took a chicken ticket, he pinched only the paper corner projecting beyond the board, arm straight and face well away. He tore off the two stubs, returned the tickets and stepped aside to make a path for the crate.

“Go on.”

The old woman went first and Taya followed close behind. She handed over her ticket; he tore it. Neither said anything more.

Slatted wooden benches ran along the carriage walls, their seat numbers painted on the backs. The front was full. Farther back, standing-ticket passengers had settled into gaps, their tool bags under their legs to keep the aisle clear.

The old woman stopped beside a bench. Her own place was empty. Of the other two places, one was empty and the other held a dozing young man.

“Excuse me. Would you move?”

He woke and shifted his legs to let her in. His eyes were closing again.

“Do sit down.”

“You’re in the chicken’s seat.”

This time his eyes opened fully. The old woman pulled a ticket from beneath a splinter, showed him the seat number and raised the crate a little. Inside, the chickens bent their heads to peck something on the bottom board.

“The chicken bought a seated ticket?”

“You can see it.”

The man looked at his own ticket without moving. One foot still pinned down his tool bag.

“One box. Wouldn’t one seat do?”

“Then refund me a ticket.”

He called towards the door for the conductor. The man came, slowing at the sight of the crate. The old woman offered both tickets and said only:

“You sold them.”

The conductor checked the seat numbers, then the short-distance standing ticket in the young man’s hand.

“You’ll have to move.”

“Give up my seat to a chicken?”

“She paid for seated tickets.”

“I paid for a ticket too.”

The conductor touched the ticket with a fingertip. STANDING was clearly printed on it, the paper creased where the man had gripped it. He bent his head, pulled the tool bag from under his foot and stood in the aisle.

For a moment the old woman could not lift the crate. He even reached out to help her support it. Together they laid it across the bench, the bottom board spanning the line dividing the seats, a little space left at either end. She sat beside it and put the tickets back.

“Don’t let them peck the seat boards,” the conductor said.

“Tell them yourself,” the man answered.

The conductor left that alone and continued checking farther along. Taya sat across the aisle. She did not help the old woman adjust the cloth again. As the wheels began to turn, the standing man swayed and caught the overhead rail.

A chicken turned round inside the crate.

“You’re comfortable enough,” he said.

One of them clucked. The old woman patted the lid and told him not to tease them. He opened his mouth, then turned to the window.

The train climbed its first slope. A gear engaged the rack between the rails, and the wooden seat carried the fine vibration into Taya’s back. She felt the money in her inner pocket: five jiao, two jiao, two jiao. She rolled the notes together and put them back.

The roofs of Lamplight Arcade slipped past. A washing line joined two high windows, wet cloth clinging to it. Someone was painting a little shop’s door frame. The owner shielded the glass with newspaper; reaching the top, the brush dislodged a flake of dry plaster. The shop had once sold clocks and later displayed shoes. Taya remembered only that both signs had used the same iron bracket.

The woman with the child sat opposite. He had woken without crying. Holding the half dry flatbread his mother had given him, he reached for the window. The wheels crossed a joint, a corner broke off onto her skirt and she had no hand free to pick it up.

Taya turned sideways and looked away from the new scratches of writing beneath the window frame. A row of freight sheds hid Lake Jingtin. When it appeared again, only a narrow strip of water showed between the roofs.

At the station in the valley a woman carrying cloth boarded. Her bundle was wider than her body and brushed Taya’s knee. Taya drew her legs in and gathered up a trailing corner of red fabric until the bundle was safely down.

“New cloth in Bridge Market?”

“Came the day before yesterday.”

“Has it gone up?”

The woman pushed an exposed bundle of thread under the cloth and looked up.

“How much are you buying?”

“Only asking for now.”

“Ask when you’re buying, then. Today’s price may not be tomorrow’s.”

Taya smiled. When the woman got off, the red cloth trailed again. Taya held it up, and the woman nodded to her over the bundle.

Outside the bone passage the train stopped longer than it had at the earlier stations. Someone below the window was checking the coupling. A heavy clunk came from beneath the carriage, then it drew taut. Continuous turning sounded from the power house beside the line. Taya moved her arm away from the window.

The short tunnel came towards them, and the pale bone outside vanished. Maintenance lamps lit the carriage wall, bright, dark, bright. The wheel noise squeezed into the narrow space and came back again beside their ears. Someone raised his voice, still telling his companion what he had been saying. A row of seats away, only a few loud endings could be heard.

Taya closed her eyes for a while. At a joint the carriage jolted twice. She tucked the slipping edge of her skirt beside her legs and did not open her eyes until the sound outside had spread out again.

Beyond the bone passage, the buildings of Bridge Market spread along an open shoulder of bone. Near roofs became other people’s streets farther away. A gallery bridge passed between two rows of windows. Somebody shook a sheet over the rail; someone below looked up and swore. The sheet stopped and slowly withdrew.

Taya stood to wait by the door and pulled the front of her clothes straight. Someone asked whether she had come to trade. She said she was looking for a person.

“What’s the name?”

The door opened. She stepped down before answering.

“Haven’t found that out yet.”

In Arcade Market Square, a cobbler lined the shoes waiting for repair beneath his stool so a customer standing about would not step into them. A newspaper boy ran between the ends of the arcade. At the Seventh Savings Bank, both doormen turned towards his cries. He moved a few paces aside without lowering his voice.

Taya slowed outside the bank.

She had been in to ask whether a small business could borrow money. The application form they gave her was thick paper, clearly printed. Of its seven spaces she had filled three, one of them with her own name. Afterwards it lay in her room for days. She used its clean back to write down prices.

Today she did not look through the doors. She followed the arcade to Clockwell Road. The sign of the Blue Bottle Pharmacy projected from an upper floor; the night school and meeting hall were farther on. Beyond them, down a few more stone steps, was Two Lamps, where she had been told to go.

The person she had asked for directions said a cook here knew the old master she was looking for. He had once worked in Lamplight Arcade, then gone to Bridge Market. He was very good at soup. As for a name, one person said it was long forgotten; another had never asked. She had also heard he was missing a finger on his left hand, only for the next person to insist both hands were whole.

From one person to another she had finally got this address. Better to ask someone who cooked than stand in Lamplight Arcade waiting for rumours.

The arcade pillars on Clockwell Road had been repaired. Their bases were neatly laid, with fresh mortar over the places freight carts had scraped white. At a shuttered shop Taya read the letting notice on the boards. Depth, rent, then a separate line: THREE MONTHS’ DEPOSIT. Rain had curled a corner. She pressed it flat with a finger, read to the end and let it go.

Bread was coming out of the oven next door. The cracks in the sour loaves showed pale gold. An assistant brought the innermost one out on a long peel and set it on the rack, making the wood tremble lightly. She had meant to ask her questions and leave. Only at the smell did she remember she had eaten nothing before coming out.

Farther down, the first smell was cooked wine.

Its harsh edge had gone, leaving a thin sweetness. Closer came softened tomato and apple pear. Chilli touched by hot oil mingled with them; the browned sweetness of onion lay about the doorway, with meat and pepper behind it. She stopped outside to breathe in. Someone carrying an empty food tin approached from behind. She moved aside, and a warm smell of beans separated out with the movement.

Two lamps were painted on the wooden board above the door. Half the paint had gone from one.

Taya held the door frame. Her right sole scraped over the threshold with a rasp. She looked down. Her left foot came down next, a slap over a raised nail head.

The person at the nearest table looked up. So did the man at the stove.

“One bean stew. Half a loaf!”

An answer came from the stove, followed by two taps of the ladle on the pot rim. Taya pulled a stool towards the long table near the door. Its leg caught in a paving joint. She freed it with her toe, drawing a long scrape across the floor.

Somebody clicked their tongue.

She looked up. The man was bent over his soup, his mouth hidden by the bowl. She sat and drew the stool in. Soon the room held only the sounds of people eating again.

Old oilcloth covered the table, drawing pins holding its corners. One pin had rusted, brown spreading into the cloth’s edge. Near the door, the table rim was worn pale where bowl-carrying sleeves had passed again and again. She placed her hand a little farther in to keep clear.

Two gas lamps lit opposite ends, one above the stove, one inside the door, their flames low beneath frosted shades. A duty rota was pinned to the inner wall beside a sheaf of accounts. Fresh figures had been added to the uppermost page. The woman taking payment kept mutual-aid meal tickets separate from loose money. Every few tickets she drew a thin tally stick from beside her and laid it over them.

The cook on duty was short and solid, apron strings sunk into his back, a wiping cloth over his shoulder. His right hand worked the ladle while his left pushed bowls towards the stove. When he wanted something passed, he tapped with the back of the ladle. An assistant lifted two bowls; someone else had already filled the gap. An apron corner brushed the fire door as they squeezed through.

Taya said nothing. First she watched how they moved.

People bumped against the door side of the room; from the far end you could not see the street. Her corner gave her half the stone steps outside. Food tins, tool bags and empty-handed people passed in turn. A woman carrying a roll of cloth looked inside and slowed, but did not come in. Soon someone else approached from the same direction and called towards the stove before even pushing the door.

She moved her elbow to let the server set down the bowl securely.

It was thick porcelain, a small chip missing from the blue rim where her mouth would not touch it. Cooked beans and potatoes sat in thick gravy, a ring of red oil on the surface. Half a loaf lay beside it, one side cleanly cut, the torn side loose with a few crumbs.

The first mouthful made her draw breath at the heat. Only after she swallowed did the sweetness of onion and beans emerge from behind the pepper. She took another spoonful. The back of the spoon pressed a potato: its corners crumbled, but the middle held together.

The cooking was well judged. She dipped a corner of bread and waited until the liquid climbed almost to her fingers before lifting it to eat. After three mouthfuls all the hunger she had been holding down came up. When someone else passed the door, she no longer put her bread down to watch.

An empty bowl was set beside the stove and carried away again. Her eyes followed it. The cook wiped a drop of oil from the rim with his shoulder cloth, passed the cloth over the stove top and reached for the next bowl.

When this rush was over, she would ask.

Towards noon more people began to come in.

Two workers sat beside Taya, their tool bags against a table leg. One wanted meat. The other stood before the blackboard for a while and came back with bean stew. A traveller carrying luggage asked where to catch the train to Lower Harbour before he had properly sat down. The cook pointed outside and told him to ask again two streets over.

“How often does it run?”

“Board at the station.”

“You live here. Don’t you know?”

The cook raised the ladle, soup dripping from it into the pot.

“I sell dinners here.”

The traveller turned to the next table. Someone there did know. He pointed with his bread and warned the man not to follow the coal carriers downhill. It was shorter on foot; with luggage to push, perhaps not. Taya listened for a couple of sentences, then drew her bowl closer to give a new arrival the corner of the table.

It was a woman with a basket of folded white cloth. Once seated, she took money from the top of her stocking and counted it before ordering. At the far end two people had textbooks spread beside their meals. One pointed to a line. The other wiped a soupy finger clean on his knee, unwilling to turn the page with it as it was.

Taya looked towards the stove. The cook had not stopped. An assistant was already at her row, three empty bowls on one hand. Seeing her glance, he reached for hers.

She held it down.

“Not finished.”

The assistant looked at the soup remaining in the bottom and moved to the next table. Taya drew her spoon through the thin skin of oil forming on top. She was about to call to the cook, then saw him lift a heavy pot and ate the spoonful instead.

Beside her the workers began talking about North Trestle Way.

“They charge on the way back empty too.”

“What’s an empty cart taking up?”

“Road. Says it counts if the wheels go over.”

The other man held the meat on his plate with bread. His knife would not cut it until he changed the angle and separated a piece.

“What if you don’t go that way?”

“Fly across.”

The speaker bent to drink, then felt for his tool bag on the floor and pulled it closer to his feet. Legs crowded beneath the table. The strap had already been kicked aside twice.

Taya asked whether they gave a ticket for the toll.

“They do.”

“Whose stamp?”

The worker looked up from his bowl.

“Why ask me? Ticket’s in the haulier’s accounts.”

“You saw which outfit took the money, surely?”

“The one that’s just taken over the platform. Great long name. It’s up on their booth. Go look yourself.”

She had delivered goods along that stretch of North Trestle Way. The outer route meant two more slopes, so people pushing carts preferred to cut through. Lately several shops had begun taking deliveries from the side; the wheel marks before their counters now pointed another way. She had heard that the owner had changed, but had yet to see a toll ticket.

“You use it too?” the worker asked.

“Used to.”

“Use it less now. Dear.”

“I want to see how they charge.”

He looked at her as though he might say more, but his companion pushed the plate towards him, offering the last piece of meat. He said he had had enough. The plate went back. Neither touched it again. The meat stayed between them, cooling fat setting around the plate’s edge.

Taya wiped the ring of soup from her bowl with bread and put it in her mouth.

A woman came to collect lunch. She handed her tin to the stove, and when it was full asked for more gravy. The cook pressed the food down with the back of the ladle and added some. The lid would not close at first. The woman held it down herself, fastened it and left contentedly, both hands beneath the tin.

An old regular at the far end never looked at the blackboard. He came in and called for the usual. An assistant moved his cap to a dry place and shifted the vinegar bottle over. Before tasting anything, the old man said today’s pot was saltier than yesterday’s.

“You weren’t here yesterday,” the cook said.

“Day before, then.”

“Different shift the day before. You’ll have to tell them.”

The regular brought his bowl beneath his nose and blew. He began talking about something his son had asked him to arrange. At first he said it had gone through; later he said they still had to wait. The person who had taken the application had gone elsewhere. The new one would not accept the old slip and told the son to come in person.

“Costs him money to get back,” the cook said.

“That’s why we’re waiting.”

The old man drank and slowly set down his bowl.

“Plenty of salt, though.”

The cook moved the vinegar bottle another half-inch towards his hand and walked away.

Taya turned her clean bowl so its chip faced the wall. The first customers left and another wave came through the door. She had meant to wait until the stove was less crowded, but every time she prepared to rise someone squeezed past. She looked at the inner wall. The last box on the rota still said EVENING SHIFT. She reckoned she could not sit here keeping somebody’s place until night.

The doorway darkened.

A tall man ducked in, turning his shoulders slightly to clear the frame. His coat was good cloth, collar and shoulders still holding their shape, but the cuffs were frayed and a pale ring of salt marked the edges. His shoes had split uppers and newly nailed soles. They landed on the paving with two solid sounds.

As he passed Taya, she smelled wet rope, old timber and salt fishiness. It was like the crates unloaded from freighters at the seaport: shut up too long, wiped outside, the same smell still in the joints.

He looked at the stove, looked at the seats and chose the nearest table.

“Two bean stews, a bowl of noodle soup, three potatoes, one salt meat.”

Without stopping his hand, the cook asked about bread.

“A whole loaf.”

The woman taking money came to write down the order. He paid the sum she named. He did not pocket his loose change straight away, leaving it on his right. Beneath the unbuttoned coat, his shirt had washed to grey. He pushed up his sleeves, exposing a ring of old marks around one wrist.

At the first bowl he cupped both hands around it and put his mouth to the rim.

The sucking and eating sounds went on without a break. Thick soup passed his lips, beans broke between tongue and teeth, his throat rose and fell, then the next mouthful followed. He drew one breath when he set down the bowl; his hand was already reaching for bread. The newly opened crumb was still loose. He tore off large pieces, one to wipe the bowl, one to press down floating beans, then pushed them into his mouth.

Somebody looked up. He did not look back.

When the noodle soup came, he pulled it close. His chopsticks tapped the rim. He lifted a thick tangle, bent his head and sucked it in. Soup splashed onto the table. He pressed bread over it, softened the bread and pushed it into his mouth with the last few beans.

The salt meat came on a small plate at his left. He moved it farther out to make room for the potatoes.

The potatoes were hot, still steaming after peeling. He wrapped one in bread and squeezed between thumb and forefinger. The floury white flesh split, soaking up the red oil left in the bowl. Before finishing the second stew he reached for another. There was food in his mouth; he paid no attention. His cheeks bulged and his jaw worked fast. Now and then he set down the bowl, smacked his lips and raised it again.

Someone at the next table clicked their tongue.

Taya’s gaze settled on his cuff. Cloth woven that tightly was something the lamp-shop owner brought out only on holidays. The worn, dirty hand beneath it moved back and forth, nails rimmed black, a few breadcrumbs clinging to the hard callus between thumb and forefinger. He used up the bread and cleaned the oil from the bottom of the plate. Even a little on his thumb went into his mouth. He had not touched the slices of meat.

At last only the meat plate remained.

He picked up a slice in his hand, bit one end and turned his face to pull. The fibres broke beneath his teeth. He tucked a little sinew hanging at his mouth inside with a finger. His jaw was still chewing; his other hand had already taken a second slice.

The tongue clicked again.

He chewed. His throat moved, and the last half-slice in his hand was gone.

The eating sounds stopped.

“Fuck you.”

By the time Taya raised her head, the back of the old coat had gone through the door.

The door swung back. Someone at the far end laid a spoon in a bowl and immediately lifted another spoonful. The woman collecting payment took the bowls from the table. The man had gathered every loose coin. The old regular looked once towards the doorway and went back to blowing on his spoon.

“Seafaring man,” the worker beside her said.

His companion nodded, drew the plate of cold meat back between them and divided it with his knife.

Taya picked up her empty bowl and went towards the stove.

The cook had just passed a basin of chopped vegetables to someone else. Most of the cloth on his shoulder was wet. He took Taya’s bowl and stacked it in a basin; standing water rocked around the rims.

“Do you know an old master from Lamplight Arcade?” she asked. “He used to make soup in Bridge Market.”

“Plenty make soup.”

“They said I could ask here.”

He looked into the dining room, supposing she had come with one of the tables. Only a few scattered customers remained. The doorway was clear. Her stool still stood at an angle outside the table; an assistant pushed it in with his leg.

“What’s his name?” the cook asked.

“Haven’t found that out. Older man. Very good at it.”

“That’s a way to find somebody.”

He turned the cloth over to wipe his hands, remembered something and pressed a lid onto another pot.

“The salty-soup fellow?”

“Salty soup?”

“I know one. Came from Lamplight Arcade. Shut two years ago. Can’t say it’s your man.”

Taya moved to a cleared corner of a table. She did not sit, but rested her hand on its edge.

“Where was the shop?”

“Along this street, second turning. Sells rope now.”

“And where’s he?”

The cook shook his head. He lifted the lid and white steam rose. When it cleared, he stirred the beans underneath. Taya waited, heat lying against her face, then moved a little aside.

“Was the soup very salty?”

“Some liked it. Good flavour.”

“Then why did he close?”

He looked towards the door and pointed out the row of shops.

“Had this habit. Once the pot was finished, he wouldn’t sell to anyone else. Told them to come tomorrow.”

“How could he sell it if it was gone?”

The cook bent his head, lifted a long-spouted kettle beside the stove and added a little hot water to his pot. The beans had reduced a good deal. He pushed them gently apart with the ladle, tasted some and added a pinch of salt.

Taya watched his hand.

“It’s bound to get weak.”

“Then season it again. A pot’s been sitting here this long, you have to replace what’s boiled away.”

“What if you can’t get it back?”

“That’s why someone has to be at the stove.”

He replaced the kettle, picked up an empty pot and told an assistant to take it out back. Taya followed it with her eyes. Beyond were chopped vegetables, a basin of soaked beans and two people crowded at the washing bench, sleeves wet to their elbows.

“He wouldn’t add any water at all?” she asked.

“He had his way. You’d have to eat it to know. I’m not talking about water.” The cook rolled the ladle in his palm. “Someone finishes work, gets to his door, it’s shut. Comes again, still shut. By the time he catches him open, he’s used to eating next door.”

Taya remembered the woman with the cloth roll who had slowed outside and walked on. She took her hand off the table edge.

“How long was he open?”

“Ten months. Owed three months’ rent when he closed. Landlord kept the tables he’d made himself too. Legs weren’t level to begin with, so he sawed them all shorter.”

“Level after that?”

“Ate there once. Bowls didn’t rattle any more. You had to stoop.”

She laughed aloud. The cook smiled too, then turned to tell an assistant not to put a hot pot straight onto the wooden bench. When he turned back, Taya asked what the shop had cost in rent.

He gave a figure. She asked again whether that was for a month. He flicked the water from both hands into the basin and looked at her.

“You thought a year?”

She touched her thumb to several knuckles of her other hand without answering.

“Want to open a shop?”

“First I want someone who can teach me.”

The cook pushed a few pieces of coal through the fire door. A pull of the poker brought a layer down; orange-red fire lit his chin. He waited for the fresh coal to catch before asking what she could cook.

“Home things. Beans, vegetables, soup.”

“For how many at a time?”

“Two or three. Sometimes more.”

“Ever cooked a pot that size?”

Taya looked beside him. The handles were blackened, with repair welds beneath both ears. Its mouth was wider than the circle she could make with her arms.

“I can learn.”

“Of course you can. Don’t rush to rent a shop first.”

He straightened and lifted his chin towards the accounts on the wall. The woman taking payment was behind him, tallying the morning’s tickets one by one beneath a thin stick. At the talk of shop rent she asked which street. When she heard it was the place now selling rope, she shook her head.

“Water’s extra there too.” She moved the top ticket aside and began counting that little stack again.

“What about here?” Taya asked.

“Stove, water, flue. Arranged years ago. Still have to mend them,” the cook said. “Cooking takes more than asking the rent. You’re in Lamplight Arcade. Look round there first.”

“Where did he go when he closed?”

“Someone saw him heading for Lower Harbour. Nothing after.”

“Didn’t take anything?”

“I didn’t follow him. His ladle’s here, though.”

He pointed the poker handle towards the stove corner.

The long-handled ladle hung above the others. Its back was worn bright, a small nick in the edge. The two rivets holding its bowl were different colours. Old cloth bound the middle of the handle, flattened where it was most often gripped. A few threads had drawn back at the edge, exposing darker wood beneath.

Taya went closer and looked up. Heat from the stove passed beneath it. Now and then the cloth end stirred. The ladle itself stayed still, its hook leaving a short vertical mark on the wall.

“Why don’t you use it?”

“Handle’s too long. No room to turn it here.”

“Could saw it shorter.”

The cook looked at her.

“Nothing wrong with it. Why saw it?”

“Would you sell it, then?”

“No.”

Her hand had already risen halfway. At the word it dropped. A strainer hung beside the ladle, its frame rewired. The new joint had not been smoothed round. She did not touch it, but drew her fingers back to her side.

“Only asking.”

“Asked quite a bit today,” the cook said.

The woman taking payment came over. Taya took her money from the inner pocket. Two jiao for the meal were drawn away and the rest pushed back. She counted it clear again and put it in her pocket, her fingers remaining at its opening a moment longer.

“I gave you the right change,” the woman said.

“I’m counting mine.”

The woman flattened the more crumpled jiao note for her, pushed it across and turned to call for an assistant to carry the dough basin for the evening shift.

At the doorway Taya called back towards the stove. “How many times do you add water to that stew?”

The cook was wiping away a small scorched spot on the pot rim.

“No set number.”

“Depends on the customers?”

“On the pot. Keep it cooking, it reduces. Busy day, it sells fast. Don’t need much more water then.”

She looked down at her toes, then at the pot, and put a hand on the door frame.

“And the flavour?”

“You ate it, didn’t you?”

Someone outside called for two meals to take away. She moved aside. A food tin passed her, and the cook’s hand was already reaching for it.

There was less light in the street. High bone shoulders and roof eaves blocked the sun, leaving only a bright patch on the wall diagonally opposite. Taya followed Clockwell Road to the second turning. Outside the third shop she first heard the dull landing of a coil of rope.

Hemp rope was piled at the entrance, a stone holding it against rolling downhill. A young man came through the door with a coil on his shoulder, his body leaning the other way. He found his footing before setting it down. Fibres rubbed his collar and stuck to the sweat at the back of his neck.

“Buying rope?” he asked.

“They used to sell soup here?”

“Used to.”

Taya looked at the door boards. A pale rectangle remained, with nail holes at its four corners. The old sign had been wider than the one hanging now.

“Do you know where he went?”

The young man shook his head. It was already storing rope when he came. The owner had changed too; he had heard only a word or two of the old business. Taya stepped two paces inside. Farther back, a curved strip remained at the foot of the brickwork, as though the stove had been removed but not scraped away completely. Two wooden storage racks stood over it now. Stock filled the window, and the room stayed dark all the way in.

“Nothing for sale back there,” the young man reminded her.

“I’m looking at the room.”

He scratched his neck, plucked a hemp fibre from the back and rolled it between his fingers.

“Want to rent, ask the owner. This one’s occupied. Next door’s empty.”

“Where’s the owner?”

“Back Street. Across the bridge. Paper about shops to let on the door.”

He went to lift another coil. Taya stepped back into the street and passed the closed door next door. A strip of floor showed beneath it. No goods inside, dust over the window, and an old pipe joint beside the wall plugged with a wad of cloth.

There was indeed a little accounts office beyond the bridge. Notices crowded the door, shop numbers, rents and permitted uses in separate columns. The last line of small print almost touched the paper’s edge. She found the place she had just seen. The monthly rent was much as the cook remembered. Below it came the deposit, water charges, repairs to the premises by separate agreement.

The person inside still had a hand on the abacus when he asked which shop she wanted.

She pointed and asked whether the price was negotiable. He reset the beads, opened a ledger and read the same figure without changing it.

“And the deposit?”

He pointed his pen at the notice. Three months. She read the line again and asked whether, if she cooked there, she could build a stove inside.

“Burning what?”

“Coal.”

“Then the flue needs inspecting first. Water has to be checked too.”

“They used to cook in this row.”

“In the rope shop. You want next door.”

He turned the ledger towards her, a finger on the two neighbouring shop numbers. Taya followed it. The rope shop had another old entry beside it. The empty one said only STORAGE.

“I’d only put one pot in,” she said.

“One pot makes smoke too. Who has to inspect it, how much needs altering, there’s no costing that yet.”

He drew a form from beside the desk and laid it between them. Taya read as far as the box for intended use, her finger still resting on the deposit in the notice. She did not reach for the form. He waited. The abacus beads stayed where they were, and he did not hurry her.

“I’ll come another day,” she said.

He took back the form and laid it on the untouched stack.

From the bridge she could see the rope store again. A worker carried a coil out. Its loose end hit the lintel, sending dust onto his cap. He took the cap off and beat it, swearing. Another coil waited behind him.

Taya did not cross back. Beside the road to the hillside station was a second-hand bookstall. She stopped before a booklet on the outer edge. HOME COOKING was printed on its cover. Its spine had frayed, exposing short lengths of stitching. When she opened it, two pages parted at an oily mark where they had stuck together.

The bean-soup page began with soaking, then stock and additions. Someone had added two lines in a pen finer than the print. The last instruction said to bring the pot thoroughly back to the boil after adding cold water. In the margin was a drawing of a pot with a blackened bottom, a sign that perhaps only its maker understood.

She read the two lines again, a thumb on the corner to keep the wind from turning it.

The stallholder reached over and gently held the spine.

“That one’s coming apart. Don’t force it open.”

“How much?”

He gave the price. Her hand paused outside her pocket. Then she smoothed the lifted page back down, shut the book and put it where it had been.

“Only looking for now.”

“Leave it there, then. Don’t lose the pages.”

A few paces away she looked back. The stallholder had laid another volume on it, a thick book corner covering the oily mark.

Few people waited beneath the shelter for the return train. A lamp had just been lit. Soot coated its shade. Standing beneath it, a face scarcely brightened; only the tickets shone yellow.

The morning’s conductor sat at the end of a bench looking at a folded paper. His fingers smoothed the crease back and forth. He opened it, looked a moment and folded it away. As Taya reached the booth, he raised his head and recognised her.

“No chickens?”

“When I can afford their tickets.”

He gave a sound through his nose, pushed the paper into his inner pocket and stood to check the signal.

“Back to Lamplight Arcade?”

“This train.”

“One jiao. Two more after this before the last.”

She passed in the jiao. A small untorn scrap remained on the ticket’s toothed edge. She did not pull at it, but carried it aboard between two fingers.

A window seat was free in the second carriage. Taya sat and leaned against the wooden back. Only then did she relax the arm she had kept tucked beside her ribs. As the train left, the railings of a gallery bridge moved past outside, one upright after another. Only the upper halves of the people above were visible. A bend hid them behind roofs.

Where the sheet had been shaken that morning, someone was now talking with an empty basket. She could not be certain it was the same person. The train was already farther off; she saw only the basket swing twice beyond the railing.

At the approach to the bone passage they stopped again. The heavy sound came through the floor and shook the seat back. Taya stretched her legs, bracing her toes in the empty space opposite. Once it darkened, maintenance lamps slid over the glass one by one. One was out, lengthening the gap.

The man opposite was asleep with his cap on his knees. When his head fell sideways it rubbed the loose window latch, and the sash knocked twice. He did not wake. Next time he leaned the same way.

Taya reached across and fastened the latch, then drew back. The sash stopped knocking. His head went on nodding. She watched a while and turned to the window.

Her reflection looked back. The little length of collar at her neck had rubbed grey. She scraped it with a nail, found it would not come clean and left it.

Another jiao paid from seven left six. In her mind she set out the afternoon’s figures again: three months’ deposit in Bridge Market, water and flue to be checked separately, eighteen and forty for the Blue Bowl in Lamplight Arcade. Remembering the soup seller’s shortened tables, she looked down at her own knees and leaned back.

The sound of the train spread out from the narrow space. She opened her eyes. The familiar roofs of Lamplight Arcade were outside. A short gleam of water showed between them, then buildings hid it again.

The mirror workers in Reflected Light Lane were finishing for the day.

The last little light left the paving and retreated up the wall. One man beneath the platform held the handwheel while another fastened the frame’s lock. Tina’s worktable had gone back inside. A small stove at her feet heated an iron. The apprentice moistened a thread at her lips and bent to put it through the needle. The woman with the child was gone. A newly washed nappy hung by the door.

The Green Glaze Bathhouse stood open. Heat and soap smell came out together. A man beside the door was putting on his shoes, wet hair against his neck. He smoothed his sock, slowly stood and first stepped aside for the person behind.

The primary-school windows were dark. Someone in the lane called for a child. Only at the third call did an answer come from far away. At the Third Nail, Mata had not yet packed up. A shoe last was held between his knees. His little hammer moved and paused among a few fine nails. His tea stood by his foot, several grains of dust floating on top.

“Back?”

“Mm.”

“How’s Bridge Market?”

“Very nice.”

He looked up and waited. When she said no more, he asked if she had found a shop.

“Wasn’t looking for a shop.”

“What did you go for, then?”

“Dinner.”

Mata pressed a nail beside the heel, his hammer held in the air.

“All that way?”

“Good dinners in Bridge Market.”

“Dear?”

“Dear.”

He nodded and drove in the nail.

“Must be very nice, then.”

Taya passed his shop and turned at the junction for North Trestle Way.

The Blue Bowl was still shut. Most of the paint had fallen from the bowl painted over the lintel. Rain had loosened another layer of the letting notice on the door, but the figures at the bottom remained clear: monthly rent eighteen yuan, deposit forty. Beneath them was Mrs Kada’s name.

She went close to the door crack and looked in by what light remained outside. The old stove position was still there, with water and drainage connections along the wall. To fit two tables in the space beside the window, she would have to leave a strip down the middle for people coming and going. She shifted sideways to see the other end, then took her hand from the door.

Cheaper than Bridge Market. But deliveries would have to climb a slope and turn across a narrow bridge. If the North Trestle route could not be used, how much would someone willing to deliver charge her? She had not managed to ask today.

A handcart came to the junction, a man pulling and a child pushing. Wooden boxes were stacked high. As a wheel crossed the drain, the top one tipped. Taya braced a hand against its corner and held it until the cart was steady again.

The man thanked her, his hands still on the handles.

“Are they charging at North Trestle Way?” she asked.

“They are.”

“How much for this trip?”

He gave the figure. Taya checked it again. It was more than she had heard at the dinner table.

“That was last week’s price,” the man said.

The child pushed. The wheel did not move. The man called to him to wait and changed his footing. Together they put their weight into it, and the cart passed the lip. At the top of the slope they stopped pushing and stood catching their breath. Taya watched the boy rub a shoe sole against a stone edge, looked back at the Blue Bowl sign and remained a little before turning home.

The stairs were darker than in the morning. The iron basin still stood against the wall. She remembered where it was and did not strike it again.

Someone was mending a stool on the turn. Its seat lay upside down across his knees. A wooden wedge had been driven into a loose joint. He tapped twice with a little hammer, stopped to pull at the leg, then tapped again. Seeing Taya come up, he asked whether she had found the person.

“No.”

“Going again tomorrow?”

She rested her hand on the polished wood at the bend in the stairs.

“Don’t know.”

He nodded and bent his head to the stool, leaving one side free for her to pass.

On the third floor Taya took out her key. Her room at No. 3 Fallen Leaf Lane faced the street. When the door opened, yesterday’s coal smoke met her in a close, dull smell. The bed touched the cupboard; a corner of cloth was caught in its door. A bowl stood upside down on the little table beneath the window. The pot lid sat crooked on the stove, resting against one side of the rim.

She pushed the door wider to let air in from the landing. A line ran between the window frame and the door frame. The towel and jacket were dry, the jacket’s shoulders hanging in two hard folds. When the close smell had thinned, she shut the door, pushed the jacket aside, drew out the chair and first took her shoes off beside the bed.

The diagonal seam on the right upper had held, but another corner had worn off the patched heel. She stretched her toes and drew them in. The floor was cool against her soles. After a while she set the shoes together.

Two doors away someone was stewing chicken. Ginger and warm fat came intermittently along the landing. She lifted the bowl on the table. Half a loaf had gone hard. She broke it in two; even the crumb inside was no longer loose.

She put one piece in the bowl with a little water and wrapped the other in its old paper.

All the money came out of the inner pocket.

Six jiao.

She peeled one overlapping note apart and laid it back. No other money was hidden between them. She had not miscounted. Of the yuan brought out that morning, one jiao had taken her there, one had brought her back and two had paid for dinner. She put the six jiao into the tin box, shut it and pushed it to the back of the drawer.

The old accounts sheet on the table had been folded twice. When she opened it the creases were furry. Rent, deposit, stove, pots, bowls, tables and chairs, coal, ingredients. A line for hired help too. She added the Bridge Market rent she had asked about today. At three months’ deposit her pen stopped on the paper.

The place in Lamplight Arcade: eighteen, forty. She dared not deduct anything for what the old stove position might save. Add pots and bowls, the first stock and money to keep going, and it was still over two hundred. She gave North Trestle cart charges a line of their own, writing only “per trip”, not the figure she had heard that afternoon.

She looked at the blank for a while and put the pen down.

Ten months. The soup man had lasted ten months.

She still did not know whether he was the master she had meant to ask about. She had seen the ladle, though. The handle really was too long, hanging above the short-handled ones. Heat passed it and only the loose end of its wrapping moved.

Hammering came through the window. Once, then again. The stool downstairs was probably still not mended. Someone asked whether it was ready. The man repairing it said to wait a bit longer.

Taya took the accounts sheet to the window to read the total. Only a layer of evening-grey light remained on the opposite wall. She had to bring the small writing close to make it out. She opened the window a crack. The repair shop was testing a new lamp. It flashed on, then went out. Someone inside held up a shade and talked to somebody else.

A pot lid sounded along the landing. Someone asked if the salt had gone in. Another person answered, the words covered by footsteps.

She folded the accounts sheet back, pinned it beneath the bowl and sat down to pick up the softened corner of bread. The smell of chicken came from next door again.

She had not lit a lamp. The window was still open.
