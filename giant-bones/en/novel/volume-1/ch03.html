Chapter Three · Ed’s Guests

In later years Morlen and Ed saw little of each other, though they still exchanged the occasional letter.

Ed had stayed at the Works Office and knew the news of their old colleagues. Someone had transferred to the water service; someone’s child had married. He wrote a little about each, then asked whether Morlen’s wages had been paid and how much longer his present job would last. One year he wrote that Feller had a grandson. Only then did Morlen remember that when he had last seen Feller’s little girl, she still had to stand on a chair to wash her hands.

Morlen had moved through several jobs in other states, and his replies came sooner or later. Sometimes he waited for a job to be settled so that he could give Ed a definite answer. By the time there was an answer, he had forgotten several of the things he had meant to say. In the reply he sent before winter last year, he had said he would come through Sertin in the spring and have a meal with him.

Returning from Hengchuan this time, he took a room at the Elm Slope Inn. The previous evening the proprietor had asked what brought him to town. He said a colleague’s funeral. The man asked no more, kept breakfast for him and explained where to change for Lamplight Arcade.

Nora recognised him sooner than he recognised her. She was busy seeing to the guests. Hearing someone call his name, she came out of the next room and said she had thought, after sending the telegram, that he might not make it in time. Morlen told her he had come from Hengchuan, had arrived yesterday and left his bag at the inn. He could go with them to Elm Slope the next morning too.

They had once had plenty to talk about. Standing together, they managed only these few things. Nora asked whether he had eaten. He said he had. While he waited for her to speak again, she had already turned to take a basket of flowers from someone.

“Go and see him first,” she said. “We haven’t started. The mirror men haven’t got it right yet. Don’t hurry away afterwards. There are some papers I’d like you to look at.”

Morlen stood beside the coffin.

A few candles burned in the room. Enough daylight came through the open door and windows to make out faces. The coffin lid leaned against the wall. White flowers stood in two glass bottles weighted with little stones. One stem bent low, its petals brushing Ed’s collar.

Ed’s hands lay outside the cloth.

His nails had been trimmed. A small dark patch remained along the side of his right forefinger. Moving closer, Morlen saw that it was not ink but a thin callus, worn there over the years. On the middle finger was a hollow where his pen had rested. With the fingers relaxed, it hardly showed.

Morlen stood by the coffin. The hand he reached out paused, then settled on its edge.

Ed had been cleanly shaved. His mouth turned down farther than it had in life, and the lines beside his nose were deeper. His brown coat had been replaced by a dark grey one. The collar lay against his neck, turned up a little on the left. Morlen remembered the brown coat he always wore. Whenever Ed lifted an arm, a thread of lining worked out at the cuff. He would pinch it, fail to pull it off and tuck it back inside.

Someone came up behind Morlen and stood beside him for a while. Neither spoke. The other man stepped back first, and Morlen followed, making room.

The people outside the window moved aside a little. A patch of light rested on half a door board. It stayed there while the mirror men found the angle again, then crossed the threshold and moved to the foot of the coffin.

A warehouse gallery ran above the roof of this house. Its projecting platform covered half the yard, and the houses opposite were tall. Even in clear weather, only a narrow strip of sky showed between the eaves. Nora had lived here for more than twenty years. She knew when to take the bedding to the outer lane for sun, which corner of the yard dried washing slowly and which flowers would not last by the window.

Ed had once bought a little plant with white flowers and put it there. After flowering, its stems grew longer and thinner. In the end they took it to Nora’s sister, where it flowered again the following year.

Today’s mirrors had been hired separately.

The uppermost stood on a platform in the outer lane where the sun reached. It caught the light and sent it to a second mirror beyond the eaves. That one stood on a frame taller than a man, with guy ropes fastened to temporary weights. The small mirror by the window turned the light into the room at last. Its surface was not large. Old boards backed it; soft leather wrapped the corners, and the brass retaining plates bore the marks of being tightened many times.

The last adjustment took the most work. The younger mirror man held the frame outside while his master went in to look, calling directions through the door. The light reached the foot of the coffin first. Raise it a little and it slipped off the white cloth. They tried several times before locking the clamp and stepping aside to see whether anything was in the way. Nora waited nearby throughout. When the master turned, she told him where she wanted it to fall.

The light did not fill the room. It came slanting through by the door, laying two bright patches on the white cloth beside the coffin and the wall behind. The weave of the cloth showed suddenly clear. A leaf in one of the bottles shone a thin green, its shadow falling beside Ed’s shoulder. A person standing by the window had one bright sleeve; the other remained in shade.

Raised a little, the light passed over Ed’s hand and reached his shoulder and neck. Nora said that would do and asked both mirror men to stay for the meal.

“Someone has to watch the one above. The sun’s moving.”

“Take turns coming down.”

The master agreed and went out to find the younger man, showing him the hand signals for their next check.

When Morlen went into the yard, the tables and chairs were in place. Two men in work coats had arrived at the gate with wrapped bread and a bag of tea. A girl carrying flowers followed them. Only when she came nearer did her face appear between the two men.

“Morlen?”

Feller shifted the tea to his other hand and held out the free one.

“You haven’t changed much.”

Morlen looked at the hair that had retreated to the back of Feller’s head.

“Neither have you.”

Feller laughed and touched his forehead.

“You never used to talk like that.”

They stood by the table. Feller now dealt with some of the issuing and returning of stores. He was still at the Works Office, though its offices had moved twice. Morlen knew which old gateway had been pulled down. Beyond that, Feller had to explain the changes by the names of shops that used to be there.

“Still taking jobs out of town?”

“Hengchuan just now.”

“A long one?”

“A few months.”

Feller nodded. A few months was good, he said, better than waiting for each separate piece of work. He passed the tea to a young man coming out of the house and asked whether they had enough chairs. Two short, the young man said. Feller took off his coat and hung it on a nail by the wall.

“I know the people next door. I’ll ask.”

Morlen followed him to the gate. More people had gathered in the outer lane. Someone pointed at the mirror frame under the eaves and asked which way the light came from. Above, the younger mirror man was lowering an empty bucket. When the rope hung into the crowd, a child reached for it and was stopped by his mother.

Taya was behind Mata, carrying the other end of a long bench.

It was too long to go straight through the gate. One end had to enter first. Mata walked backwards, stopping his heel before the threshold. Taya raised her end. Morlen took hold of the seat and helped turn it. The back of her hand brushed his cuff. She drew it away at once and changed her grip to the leg.

“Inside?” Mata asked.

“There’s room by the wall.”

Taya worked round the table corner, set the bench firmly down and straightened, looking into the house first. She had seen Ed a few times when she delivered things for the family. Each time he asked which way she had come back, then began telling her the shortcuts he used when he was young. Some were closed now. She had told him, but next time he would tell her again.

She went in and stood by the coffin for a while.

When she came out, Nora put a basket of bread into her hands and asked her to cut it. Taya tried the knife, took a piece of bread and laid it crust down, pressing it steady before she cut.

Several people outside were still looking at the mirrors. A woman selling small sundries came to the gate and asked whether someone was having a wedding. When told, she hurriedly moved her little basket behind her, asked the name quietly and went in too.

A child put his hand in the bright patch on the ground and spread his fingers. Its shadow fell on the wall. He turned his palm over and back until his mother caught his wrist and led him aside. A little later the light reached her shoe. She did not move again.

An old woman came out with reddened eyes. Seeing the benches in place, she felt the seat before sitting. The person beside her asked how Ed had gone.

“At home,” she said.

“Not at work?”

“At home. He’d taken leave that day.”

She folded her handkerchief. It came out too small, and she opened it again.

“That afternoon he was still talking to Nora about getting the house sorted out when they had time. He was sitting there, and suddenly something was wrong. They sent for the doctor, but they couldn’t save him.”

The other person asked no more and shifted a little farther along to give her room.

Feller brought back two chairs. One had once been discarded by the Works Office; a faded number remained on its back. He spotted it, scraped off a little dry paint with his finger and called Morlen over.

“Ed had one like this. Everyone else said it dug into them. He insisted it didn’t wobble.”

“His had a short left leg,” Morlen said.

“And he still wouldn’t change it.”

“He put paper under it.”

Feller bent to look at the feet of this chair and straightened, laughing.

“You even remember that.”

Someone in the yard mentioned how slowly Ed sorted things. Someone else said there was something to be said for slow: leave it with him and he could always find it. Feller waved a hand.

“He could find it, but you had to wait. When he said ‘a moment’, never believe him.”

Morlen smiled too.

When Ed said a moment, there was often one more small thing before him that had to be done. The first time Morlen finished a day with him at Fir Trestle Warehouse No. 7, the eating house was almost closed and Ed was still on his knees, spreading out wet sheets of paper.

Ed’s hair had not been white then.

He wore an old raincoat too large for him. When he crouched, its skirts trailed on the floor. The afternoon rain had stopped, but water still dripped from the warehouse gallery roof. The temporary reinforcement stood nearby, fresh-cut timber pale yellow beside the damp marks that would not yet leave the foot of an old post. The workers had taken the handle off the winch. Their foreman had hurried the two men twice. When they stayed, he left the key on the sill and told them to close up behind them.

Morlen’s inspection book was held between boards of hardwood and already in his leather bag. What had got wet were the stores issue-and-return sheets. Several sacks of old fastenings had been brought down through the rain, and water dripping from their mouths had soaked a broad patch of the list. With a letter knife Ed worked apart the stuck edges and spread the sheets beneath the window one at a time.

“Dry them when you get back,” Morlen said.

“Fold them up again and they won’t come apart when we get there.”

“Put something between them, then.”

“Find me something.”

Morlen went round the room. The empty packing cases held only a few scraps of thick paper smeared with oil. The cloths left on the shelf were black and stiff. He picked out a thin board that was a little cleaner and brought it over.

Ed felt it with his fingertips, turned it over and shifted one sheet onto it.

“Can we go?”

“Not this one yet.”

Morlen looked outside. Water ran down the stone steps to the street. A little later and the eating house would be banking its fire. Since breakfast he had eaten only half a piece of bread, and his stomach felt tight with emptiness. Ed had unfastened his raincoat. He took a pencil from his shirt pocket and copied the few figures still legible onto another, dry sheet.

“You go first,” he said.

“Who’ll lock up?”

“I will.”

“And hand it in when we get back?”

“I will.”

Morlen stood for a while, then put his leather bag back on the sill.

Without lifting his head, Ed asked, “What else do you want?”

“Nothing.”

“Then stop standing in the light.”

Morlen moved aside. Ed held the paper at an angle towards the window. A fine line emerged within the washed-out ink: there had been two rows underneath. He leaned closer, crossed out the figures he had copied and wrote them again.

The warehouse grew quiet except for water dripping from the eaves. Morlen found a place to sit beside a bundle of materials not yet returned. Only after sitting did he discover an unstripped patch of bark behind the timber, caught in his coat. As he eased the cloth free, Ed laughed over by the window.

“You might look before you sit.”

“You might hurry up.”

Ed lifted a sheet towards the window, carefully supporting the wettest part with his wrist. The sun came out through a little clear sky after the rain. Light shone through the paper and showed the fingers beneath. He turned it over, laid it against the dry board and at last stood up.

Half the eating house’s shutter boards were up.

They went through the half still open. The cook was scraping his pot. When they asked for a meal, he first showed them what was in it. A thick layer of wheat grains and meat juices clung to the bottom. The scraper left a clean black track, with a little softened onion beside it.

“Eat this?”

Both nodded.

The cook loosened the scrapings with hot water, filled two bowls and took a length of bread from under a cloth. Ed counted out his money. Halfway through, his fingers stopped in his pocket. He felt round again.

“Pay mine for now.”

Morlen paid.

“Give it back tomorrow.”

“When have I ever short-changed you?”

“I’ve never lent you anything before.”

Putting down the bowls, the cook told them to eat first if they never had; they could settle their accounts tomorrow. They sat at a little table by the door. Ed turned his raincoat over the chair back with the inner pocket facing in, then moved the board holding the lists away from the soup.

There was not much in the hot soup, but plenty of bread. Morlen was wiping the rim of his bowl with it when Ed said, “There may be supper left at home.”

Morlen stopped.

“May be?”

“I said I’d be home to eat.”

“Why didn’t you say so before?”

Ed raised his bowl and took a drink before answering.

“In case there wasn’t.”

Morlen swore at him. Ed did not answer back. He broke the bread and pushed the larger half across.

They went to Ed’s house afterwards all the same.

On the way Ed kept saying there might not be any food. Once they reached the door, he told Morlen not to begin by saying they had eaten. Morlen asked why. If Nora had cooked, Ed said, they would have to eat a little more.

The doorway had been narrower then. Ed turned his shoulder to get the board in his arms through first, taking his raincoat off outside. There was a smell of stewed beans in the house. A child had fallen asleep at the table with his head on his hands. Nora came out, took Ed’s soaked coat and asked why they were so late.

“The papers got wet.”

“And you?”

Ed looked down at his reasonably dry shirt.

“I didn’t.”

Nora turned the coat round and tipped a pouch of water out over the threshold.

“Supper,” she said.

Ed went straight to fetch the bowls.

Morlen stood by the door. Looking at the table that had already been laid and half cleared again, he could only take off his hat.

“They’re about to begin inside.”

Feller touched Morlen’s arm. People at the door were moving back to let the older ones in. Morlen turned sideways with a hand on a chair back and found a place where the flower basket had stood.

The mirror man above had adjusted it once more. The bright patch had shifted a little farther back, reaching Ed’s shoulder and the white cloth beside his head. The room was full now. Those arriving later stayed in the yard. When the doorway would hold no more, some looked through the window. The people in front took off their hats and held them against their chests.

Ed’s son spoke first.

It was the youngest, not yet fully grown, his wrists showing beneath black sleeves as though he were a size too small for the clothes. Morlen had seen him moving chairs outside that morning and had not recognised him. At his father’s year of birth, the boy looked down to find the line on the paper. An older woman beside him prompted him quietly, and he went on.

Feller brought a few words from their colleagues at the Works Office. At first he raised the paper towards the light. Then he simply lowered it and said that, with Ed gone, they had better not all come to him when they wanted those old account books.

Someone laughed very softly.

“And if you borrowed anything, give it some thought over the next few days,” Feller said. “He kept a note.”

Nora lowered her head. The corner of her mouth moved. Feller saw it, picked up the paper again and slowly read out the few sentences on it.

A cart went along the outer lane, iron wheels sounding on the paving. The people inside stayed still until it had passed. Then they could hear that Feller’s last words concerned the arrangements for Elm Slope the next morning. Some would go with the family; some had work. They asked quietly when to leave.

Nora went to the coffin to flatten Ed’s left collar point.

She had tried once that morning. The stiff interlining made it spring back. Bracing her thumb beneath it, she eased the coat out towards his shoulder with her other hand. Bent over him, she blocked the view of several people outside the window.

“You in front, would you mind moving? We can’t see.”

Her hands stopped.

Morlen stood close enough to see the wrinkles on the backs of her hands, patches of them whitened by hot water. She looked back at the people between the door and the window without resting on any one face.

“One moment,” she said.

She turned the collar point up again, slipped her fingers beneath it, straightened a crooked seam and laid it back. This time it stayed flat. She passed her hand over the shoulder once more before slowly straightening and going back beside her son.

No one outside the window spoke again.

After a while Nora turned to Morlen.

“Will you say something too?”

He had prepared nothing. He looked into the coffin, his fingers touching a coat button.

“We ate supper together twice,” he said.

Feller looked up.

“On the same day,” Morlen added.

The people by the window laughed. Morlen told the story. When he reached Ed telling him not to say they had already eaten, Nora broke in.

“He came home and told me you hadn’t.”

Morlen looked at her.

“I didn’t like to ask either,” she said.

This time the laughter spread from the room into the yard. Feller asked whether he had managed the second supper. Morlen nodded. He had, and taken another spoonful. Someone said that was being young; who could do it now? The person beside him said he need not include himself in that.

Nora laughed too. She wiped beside her nose with her handkerchief. Lowering it, she looked at Ed.

Morlen remembered other things, but they would not come out as easily. There had been many more meals, some on worksites, some at little stations on their travels. Later he had come back to Sertin less often. Their meetings were hurried, with time only to ask how things were. Same as ever, Ed said. Morlen answered in the same way.

He took his hand from the button.

“That’s all I have.”

Nora nodded to him.

At the door someone set a child down. Unsteady on his feet, he clung to the adult’s leg. Another group had arrived outside, treading softly. They waited until there was room, then came in carrying their flowers.

Later the tables were brought into the yard.

The one that had stood by the wall was not enough. They fetched another from the back room and covered the gap between them with a clean cloth. Benches ran down both sides. Anyone able to hold a bowl could eat standing. A passage still had to be kept clear into the house. As people talked, they no longer kept looking that way.

Nora’s sister had put the meat on early, adding potatoes and several large pieces of cabbage. When the lid came off, steam gathered under the lintel and the smell of meat broth reached the outer lane. Someone went home for a jar of pickled cucumbers. Someone else brought half a barrel of weak beer: it would have to be drunk anyway, so everyone might as well have some.

After cutting the bread, Taya helped serve. The spoon was too shallow. Meat kept slipping off its edge, so she found a fork to use with it and brought up the last pieces from the bottom too. Nora told her to eat first. Taya said she would when she had filled these few bowls. As she handed one over, another empty bowl arrived in front of her.

“Stop for a moment,” Mata said.

He took the spoon from her hand, put it by the pot and pushed a filled bowl towards her.

Taya sat on a low stool with the bowl on her knees, bracing the rim with her bread. A piece of potato was still hot. When she bit it open, steam rose from the break. She blew on it. Behind her, someone asked who would help carry in the morning. She drew her legs aside to let the person going to answer get past.

Morlen sat with Feller at the end of the table. The bowls and cutlery did not match. The bowl before him was thin, with a faded blue-green line round its rim. Feller recognised it and said it was probably from next door. He had once been to a celebration there; the family even drank water from bowls like these.

They talked about people they used to work with. One had transferred to the water service; one had opened a small workshop. Morlen remembered another name but kept putting someone else’s face to it. Only when Feller described the man’s walk did he remember.

“Did his leg get better?”

“It did. He dances now. Better than me.”

Feller set a meat bone aside in his bowl and was about to go on when someone called him from behind. He turned to answer a question about a batch of returned materials. It took more than a sentence or two. He pulled a pencil from his pocket and wrote an address on the back of some bread wrapping. Even then the man asked whether he could go tomorrow.

“Yes. Don’t go today. I’m here today.”

The man smiled his thanks and went away with his bowl. Feller put the pencil back and bent over his potatoes, which were no longer very warm.

“What were you asking?”

Morlen shook his head, then remembered and reminded him about the leg.

A small plate stood by the gate with money and a few folded papers in it. Some people arriving stopped there; some found Nora and put things straight into her hands. A man carrying a tool bag arrived late and empty-handed. He apologised first: he had been unable to change his money that morning and would bring it when he passed tomorrow.

Nora asked him to eat first.

Instead he went to the back, lifted an empty pot down from beside the stove and asked where the hot water was. Halfway through washing, his sleeve slipped. Unable to spare a hand, he asked someone nearby to roll it up. The woman who did so brushed a little dust from his shoulder too, saying he brought the worksite with him whenever he came.

Morlen had finished and was looking for somewhere to put his bowl when Nora came over from the door and laid a hand on his.

“A little more.”

She took the bowl and added some. Watching the spoon in her hand, he asked whether she had eaten.

“I have.”

Her sister said from behind her that she had taken two mouthfuls. Nora looked round, surrendered the spoon and sat at the table. Someone passed her bread. She took it, broke off a corner and put it in her mouth. Someone recalled the last time Ed had eaten next door. He would not leave for ages, and finally admitted that he had forgotten his key.

“He did go home to look for me,” Nora said.

“Did he find you?”

“I was asleep inside.”

The people at the table laughed. She laughed too. In the end their youngest had opened the door for him through the back window, she said. Father and son had felt round the kitchen for something to eat and finished a plate of pickles meant for the following day.

“He always said he didn’t eat much.”

She looked down for the corner of bread in her hand, only to find she had eaten it. She broke another piece from the larger one.

When the younger mirror man came down for his meal, his master had kept a place for him. He sat on the low stool by the door, his knees above the table edge, and had to hold his bowl up. Both ate quickly. The master put his bowl down first and went to check the light. Once the sun moved a little farther west, the wall above would shade the mirror. They would have to start packing up.

The brightness remaining in the yard touched the leg of a bench. An old man sat down there with his tea and spread his hands on his thighs, backs upward. Answering someone beside him, he slowly turned both hands over.

The guests began to leave.

Feller had to call at the Office and would return the next morning. He picked up his coat and found a sleeve. Halfway into it, he discovered an extra key in his pocket. After a moment he remembered the chairs borrowed from next door. Morlen helped him take two back. The householder was washing a child’s hair. Across the basin, they were told to set them down. There was no hurry about the other one; if it was needed tomorrow, it could stay.

When they returned, a woman outside the gate asked whether she could take a little leftover bread. Nora wrapped some, then added another piece and asked her to take a portion to the neighbour at the lane entrance whose legs troubled her. The woman opened her basket and had the wrapped bread put on top so that it would not be crushed.

A man in a finely checked coat reached the mouth of the lane holding his teacup. His companions had already turned the corner. He hurried a few steps, looked down at the cup and put it in his bag before following them.

Taya was coming out with a stack of empty bowls. All she saw was the coat disappearing round the corner. Mata asked whether she had left anything of her own. She checked the windowsill and rolled her sleeves down.

She helped carry the long bench back to the shoe-repair shop, then went off with Mata. Passing the gate, she turned and told Nora she would see her tomorrow. Nora freed a hand to wave.

The younger mirror man turned the mirror over, wrapping its lower corners first and working upwards. Inside, the bright patch left the white cloth, travelled a little way along the foot of the wall, rested on the half-open door and was gone. The master removed the clamp by the window, checked the gap for a leather pad that might have fallen into it, found it and put it back in a compartment of the wooden case.

The money Feller had left was in an unsealed envelope. Nora added the balance agreed that morning. The master counted it in front of her and put it in his inner pocket, then checked that they would not be wanted to set up again tomorrow. She said no. They would be leaving first thing.

The two men loosened the wooden frame section by section. The youngest son came to help. The master showed him where he could hold it and where it would pinch his hands. They lowered the upper mirror slowly by rope, pausing as it passed the eaves. Several people in the yard waited with their heads lifted. Only when the wooden case was safely down did they go their separate ways.

Nora asked the mirror men to take the remaining bread. The younger one looked at his master. The master accepted the paper parcel, said thank you and untied the last rope at the yard entrance for her.

Once the house had emptied, it was cooler than when Morlen first came in.

Several chairs had been returned to their places. The door was still open. The white cloth by Ed’s shoulder had regained its ordinary colour. The candles still burned with low flames, the one nearest the door bending now and then. Nora’s sister sat in the outer room folding used cloths. Her youngest son wiped the tables in the yard. Water squeezed from the cloth’s corners and ran along the grain to the ground.

Nora asked Morlen into the back room.

She first moved a sewing box from the table, then lifted a bundle to uncover a wooden chest. It had stood beneath the window, the side against the wall darker than the rest. She did not open it straight away. First she asked Morlen to move towards the door so that the light outside could come in.

Morlen moved aside.

“He always wanted a taller cupboard,” she said. “It’s damp down there. No place for paper. I said shelves would do, but he said they’d get dusty.”

The catch opened on several piles already sorted. Personal letters near the top had been tied separately with cloth tape. Nora lifted them out and set them behind her. The packets underneath had different covers: thick works paper, old envelopes opened and turned over, and one piece of stiff binding cut from an account book.

“They’ve already collected what belonged to the Office,” she said. “These are the copies he kept, and his usual notes. I didn’t like to throw them all away. I recognise some of the names. You’re in here too.”

Morlen took the top packet.

The string had not been pulled tight. At each corner a scrap of thick paper cushioned it underneath. When he opened it, the sheets were dry, with a faint smell of old paper pulp. Materials lists came first. Several sheets behind them recorded food and lodging on work journeys, their dates far apart. One item had its original price crossed out, two words added and the figure beside it revised downwards.

“He said they might still be needed.” Nora picked up another packet. “I asked for how long. He couldn’t say.”

She pulled out a chair and sat on the edge of the bed herself. Morlen sat too, reading the covers of the packets first without opening each one.

Ed’s handwriting had grown smaller. He filled the paper but always kept the binding margin clear, its holes neatly punched. Morlen had only heard of several projects. Another packet he knew. The cover was carefully lettered: Fir Trestle Warehouse No. 7.

He moved it in front of him.

Nora noticed.

“Wasn’t that already investigated?”

“It was.”

“He sorted through these a few times afterwards. Once he couldn’t find a sheet and spent half the night looking.”

Morlen loosened the paper cord and lifted the first sheet.

It was a list of the copies and extracts that followed. Against several entries Ed had added the numbers of the originals. The ink varied in shade; they were probably not all added on the same day. The second sheet copied the conclusion of an acceptance report, with Morlen’s name copied at the bottom.

May remain in use.

A broad space had been left above those words. He looked at it for a while, set it down and turned to the records behind. Pencil lines ran along the margins, with short notes from different dates between the sheets. On one page several hurried lines were his own. Another gave the names of people involved at the time, with little brackets in which Ed had added what each had handled.

Morlen put the sheets back in their original order.

“I’ll take these and look through them.”

Nora did not answer at once. She picked up the list, waited until he showed her which packet, then found a stub of pencil by the table and noted it on the paper she was keeping.

“Do they have to go back to the Office?”

“I’ll check the numbers first. They have the originals. Some of these need to be read alongside them.”

He had meant to leave town tomorrow. He told Nora he would stay an extra day and make the enquiry before he left.

“Keep them if they’re useful. Don’t get his letters mixed in.”

Even after saying it, she took the packet back and checked it through, making sure there were no letters inside before returning it to him.

Morlen asked whether Ed had mentioned the matter again at home. Nora thought for a while. He had sometimes said a little about it, but she could not remember exactly what. Later the papers had been moved. She had complained that the chest was in the way and quarrelled with him over it. Not over this packet: all the things were piled there, and they had to be moved before she could open the cupboard.

“That very day he said it was time to sort it out.”

She looked at the space left in the chest, moved the other papers towards it, then stopped and put them back.

“I hadn’t meant you to take them today. Since you were here, I thought I’d ask.”

“It’s all right.”

Outside, someone asked where to put the flower basket for the morning. Nora answered, telling them to leave it where it was until she came out.

Morlen wrapped the papers again, cushioning the corners with the original scraps of thick paper. Nora took an old cloth from the bottom of the chest and asked him to put it round the packet. It would offer some protection if it rained. He said he had brought a bag. She handed him the cloth all the same, telling him he could take it off when he got there.

They stood for a while in the back-room doorway.

“At home,” Morlen asked, “did he ever say he felt unwell?”

“Some days he said he was tired. The doctor told him to rest, so he took leave.”

“And that day?”

“Nothing much. After lunch he said he didn’t want to lie down all afternoon. I told him to sit a while and put his tea beside him.”

She looked towards the corner of the table in the outer room. A flower basket stood there now. Only a little bare edge of wood remained where the teacup had been.

“He never drank it.”

Morlen asked no more.

Nora’s youngest came to fetch her, carrying a wrung-out cloth. She took it and told him to rest for a while, only then remembering to introduce the two. The young man held out a damp hand to Morlen. Feeling the paper cord against Morlen’s palm, he loosened his grip a little.

Morlen said he had seen him as a child.

The young man smiled and said he did not remember.

“You were asleep,” Nora said.

She draped the cloth over a chair back and went with Morlen to the gate. A small handcart passed through the space where the mirror frame had been, its baskets rocking. The man pushing it stopped to ask whether he could carry anything for her in the morning. He and Nora began discussing the stretch of road they would take.

Morlen waited until they had finished, then asked what time he should come in the morning.

“Half past seven,” she said. “The door will be open. Just come in.”
