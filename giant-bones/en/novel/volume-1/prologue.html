Prologue · The Elena

In Lower Harbour, noon had to pass through a cannon.

At the appointed hour the watchmakers came out of their shops, a few newly repaired pocket watches cupped in their hands, and waited at the door. Dockers made their sacks secure; a baker’s assistant put his head out from beside the oven. An old woman on Lakeside Street, too deaf to hear it herself, watched the cat on the windowsill opposite.

The cat always jumped down when the gun went off.

On days when the Elena stood harbour duty, it was her responsibility. When she was out on patrol, the shore signal station took over. The shipyards, ferry landings and warehouses all had clocks of their own, and the harbour office checked the time by telegraph every day; still, the people on the waterfront liked to hear the gun. A clock could hide behind a shopkeeper’s counter. A gunshot went over the counter, straight to the ears of the men carrying sacks.

That morning, in early April of Concord Year 614, smoke was rising from the Elena well before it was needed.

The lake port had been open again for less than a month. A few planks on the pier had been replaced, their fresh-cut edges bright among the old timber. Along the shipyard shore lay two newly painted barges. The water beside them was clear and cold; the morning mist had not quite lifted. The sun had reached the opposite bank, but the stone steps near the harbour still held the chill of the night.

Fenn stood on the pier for a moment and shifted his bag to the other hand.

The lake was darker than it had looked in the recruiting magazine. Close to shore it was grey-green; beyond the shadows of the piles it shone a cold blue. Little waves pushed into the stone steps, left scraps of vegetable leaf and cork, and drew away. The mooring posts had been rubbed black. Water from the night lay in their tops, reflecting the sky and the name that hung above the water on the warship’s stern.

Elena.

The brass letters were fastened separately above a narrow band of white paint. From a distance the paint looked neat; up close, Fenn could see the repairs, a little brighter than the rest, the brush marks still showing. Lower down, a dark seam ran along the grey-blue hull at the waterline. Old rope fenders lay between the ship and the quay, flattening and easing apart with the waves.

She was not a large ship. A short, thick funnel stood between two low deckhouses, blackened at the top and painted pale grey below. The bridge rose a little higher, with narrow windows that could be propped open. Flag halyards and signal lines ran from the crossbar above it; even those lines had looked particularly smart in the magazine. Boats hung on either side, patched boards showing along their bottoms.

A gun stood on the bow beneath heavy canvas, its muzzle pushing a blunt, rounded shape into the cloth. Near one side of the stern another mounting was enclosed by a folding rail. Several closely set barrels and part of a crank handle showed beneath its cover. Fenn recognised a hand-cranked Gat gun. He had seen one during shore training. The armoury instructor kept it polished, but before allowing the recruits to come close, he made them put their hands behind their backs.

The first sound the warship made came from her belly.

One beat after another, at steady intervals. Nearby a water pump gave a brief, rapid tremor; now and then a pipe tapped softly, or a shovel struck an iron edge. The main engine was not yet turning the propeller. The water astern lay quiet. Only a stream of warm water flowed from an outlet in the hull, spreading a veil of mist over the cold lake.

The sailor on watch at the end of the pier looked up.

“Papers.”

Fenn handed over his posting order, folded into quarters.

The sailor wore a short indigo jacket with a square, pale-grey collar turned over the neck, two fine white lines stitched around its edge. The cloth was thick, worn shiny at the shoulders and elbows, though the cuffs were very tidy. A clean light-coloured cover fitted over his round cap. On the black ribbon around it, silver-grey letters read SEVENTH WATER GUARD. Two short tails hung down behind his head, touching his collar in the wind.

Fenn glanced at his own cuffs.

His new clothes still smelled of warehouse mothproofing and coarse wool. The seams held hard creases. The man issuing uniforms had found his size in a ledger: the trousers fitted at the waist, but the sleeves were much too long. Fenn had put a few stitches in them at the temporary lodgings the night before. They looked all right while he stood still; when he raised his hands, the left one came up shorter than the right.

The sailor finished reading the order and checked his name.

“Engine-room trainee?”

“Yes.”

“Find the boatswain once you’re aboard. Don’t block the ladders with your luggage.”

As the man stepped aside, Fenn got a clear look at his rifle.

It was the short version of the Lakeguard service rifle: blackened steel, a tea-brown stock, and a few lines in the fore-end rubbed deeper by the palm. The sling was broad and old, with a dull sheen where it rested on the shoulder. A plain, straight magazine projected below the action, its edges rounded off. Beside the bolt were the arms factory’s stamp and a clearly struck serial number.

The rifle held fifteen rounds. After each shot the action still had to be worked by hand; however many cartridges were inside, they did not spare the rifleman that movement. The instructor had written the two facts separately on the blackboard. Recruits from different places knew different rifles. Some were used to levers, others to long stocks. At a Sertin armoury, they still drew their weapons by the local numbers and kept them by the local rules.

The watchman also wore an issue knife at his waist. Its blade was hidden in a brown leather sheath, leaving only the dull metal guard exposed. Repeated rubbing had worn a pale ring around the sheath’s mouth. Fenn had seen plenty of knives on the waterfront: for cutting fish, opening sacks, shaving wooden wedges. Worn on a military belt beside a rifle and a document case, this one marked its owner’s occupation very clearly.

A wooden board beside the pier carried separate columns of rules for weapons and visitors. The people bringing vegetables, repairing the gangway or collecting payments all stopped beneath it. A craftsman delivering parts for the shipyard gave his name; the watchman found him in the register and let him aboard with his tools. When someone tried to bring along a helper hired on the spot, the sailor half-closed the book and asked who the helper was.

With his posting order in his hand, Fenn went up the gangway.

The deck gave a small movement that the pier had not. As he had been taught ashore, he stopped, saluted the flag at the stern, then turned towards the officer of the watch.

He straightened his back and brought his right hand to his cap, fingers together, palm angled down. His new cuff promptly slipped over half the back of his hand.

The officer returned his salute.

“Fenn?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Get your berth assigned after you report. Don’t leave your bag here.”

Fenn bent to pick it up, his cap ribbons brushing his ear. Someone beside him caught the bottom of the bag just before it struck the rail.

“Don’t nail your feet down quite so hard.”

The man wore a dark-blue cap with a short peak, two narrow silver stripes at the cuffs, and clean black shoes worn thin around the edges. Fenn recognised the boatswain’s insignia and began to straighten up again.

“No need this time. Just keep your balance while you’re carrying things.”

The boatswain’s name was Haller. He looked at Fenn’s uneven sleeves, then at the bag. He did not laugh.

“Ashore, the ground helps you stand. On a ship, leave yourself a little knee.”

He led Fenn forward along the deck. His uniform was the same as the watchman’s, only older at the collar and mended at the buttonholes. Even the way the fabric had softened seemed to fit his movements. Passing the bridge, he stopped to salute. His hand moved cleanly; his gaze rested steadily on the man receiving it. Then he went on walking. Watching him, Fenn felt that the movements they had counted out on the parade ground had suddenly found their proper length.

A grey-blue Water Guard flag flew from the mast. Its silver arch opened in the morning wind, then folded back around the halyard. Other small identification flags were rolled on a rack near the bridge; beneath them hung a string of signal boards polished by many hands.

“We’re patrolling the east shore this afternoon. A few lights to check; back before dark,” Haller said. “First learn your way around the ship. You don’t touch the main engine whenever you feel like it. Someone will show you. For now, get your berth.”

Fenn followed him down the ladder.

Inside were the smells of warm oil, damp cloth and soap from a freshly washed floor. Steam pipes bound in insulating tape ran along the bulkheads, their joints neatly wrapped, small labels fixed at intervals. If two men met in a passage, one had to give way. The sailors did it easily, turning a shoulder without breaking stride.

In the mess deck a row of hammocks had already been lashed into neat rolls. Numbered lockers stood below them, boots lined up beneath. Someone had tucked a photograph from home inside a locker lid. The lid stood half-open, and only half the child’s face could be seen.

Fenn found his number.

There was room for spare clothes, writing paper and his few books. His engine diagrams, folded shut, went on top. The binding had worked loose; he had glued it back with cloth at home. Beside him a sailor was sewing a button on an inside-out work coat. Seeing the books, he moved his sewing box a little farther over.

“New aboard? Don’t put clean clothes against that board. It gets damp there sometimes.”

Fenn thanked him and moved them as he suggested.

A telephone rang in the next compartment. A man in a dark-grey work coat went quickly to answer it, lowering his voice. The instrument was mounted on a black-painted wooden box, a brass crank on the right rubbed pale at the handle. The crank rang the bell; a small battery for speaking sat in a separate drawer inside. Two wires in woven insulation ran overhead and passed through little porcelain sleeves at the bulkhead.

There was a telephone in Fenn’s home town too, at the postal agency. You paid for a call, and the operator first had to find the person you wanted. If they were out in the fields, you waited. The ship’s lines were much shorter: the engine room, bridge and duty stations all lay within the same hull. But once a door was shut, machinery and water could still put a great distance between people.

Someone asked a question at the other end. The man in the work coat answered in a few words, replaced the receiver, wiped his wet hands on a cloth and turned to lift a toolbox.

Haller gave Fenn a form to take ashore.

“Learn the way back first,” he said.

The form had separate entries for boiler water, drinking water and loading time. The two kinds of water used different pipes, and the signatures below them were different too. Fenn had tended a steam pump for someone in the country; he recognised the boiler-water column. He folded the paper, put it in his pocket, and stepped aside for the man carrying the toolbox as he went out.

* * *

Beyond the naval pier, the harbour opened out.

A stretch of quay separated the coal berths from the fresh-produce docks. Black barges sat deep in the water, their coal heaps half-covered with tarpaulins. The exposed pieces caught the sunlight along little wet edges. A steam crane ashore lowered an iron grab. As the cable tightened, the pulleys in the timber frame sounded first, followed by the heavy grinding of the winch. The grab rose slowly, swung over the rails and emptied into a wagon. The load struck the wagon floor with a dull crash. The men nearby turned their faces away.

Farther along, the air smelled of fish, wet wood and newly opened grain. Several grain barges lay side by side, their low gunwales almost level with the landing boards, sacks stacked from the hold openings to the edge. A crate of machinery from a Whitefurnace trading house hung beneath another derrick, its centre of gravity and lifting points marked on the sides. Bundles of timber from the Seven Necks still wore their dark bark; the cut ends were fresh. A worker crouched beside them, testing a binding strap with his fingers.

The dockers spoke in different accents, but their ways of counting sacks were much alike. Every few calls someone turned over the little wooden tally in his hand. A row of weights stood outside the weighhouse. The largest took two men to lift, and a lead seal from the Bureau of Weights and Measures rested on the base. A foreman swore at a wagon that had not yet been cleared. When the steam crane changed its rhythm, he looked up at once and held the nearest man half a step back.

There were more boats on the lake than there were berths.

Wooden sailing boats had their canvas gathered beneath the spars, their masts drawing rows of crooked reflections in the water. Iron-hulled tugs sat low beneath thick funnels, towing two or three barges at a time. When a tug had rounded a bend, its barges were still slowly turning through the water it had left. The ferry’s upper deck was open, windbreak cloth stretched between the rails. People with vegetable baskets sat there, gathering the baskets close around their feet. An upstream passenger-and-cargo boat had a row of square windows. Beneath them, a little paint had flaked away to show an older colour.

Fenn stopped to let a group of handcarts pass.

Their wheels bumped over the joins in the paving, making the crates rattle together. A girl sat on the last empty cart, holding a pot of flowers tightly in her arms. The man pushing it told her to keep her feet tucked in. He still made a small detour around a puddle.

Navigation marks slanted out into the lake, smaller and smaller, until in the thin mist they were only dark points. Far-off sails moved slowly between them. A gust came, and the light on the water changed.

The chart beside the quay showed all of Lake Jingtin. Fenn found the little anchor for Lower Harbour and followed the waterway towards the Qiting outlet, marked a little over forty kilometres away. From there the Shilan River wound through shoals, bridge openings and moorings before reaching the Grey Sea at the other end of the chart. That stretch was more than four hundred kilometres. Notes on coaling and waiting for the tide crowded both sides, some printed so small that he had to lean close to read them. Deep-draught vessels stayed at the river mouth. Their cargo went into smaller boats and travelled inland by stages.

The Elena’s broad, flat bottom and shallow draught had been made for these waterways. The register called her a Water Guard gunboat. Patrolling the lake, inspecting the river and watching the lights at its mouth were all part of her duties. Longer assignments meant nights at moorings along the way; work in coastal waters needed a separate reckoning of weather and supplies. Before going ashore, Fenn had seen Haller checking the duty book. Today’s lights were listed on one page. Only after he turned it did the moorings and coal for another trip down the Shilan appear.

The water office stood beside the freight yard.

Fenn passed the form through the window. The clerk checked the ship’s name, took down a loading ledger and pointed to a column for his signature. He wrote his name slowly. When he had finished, the clerk tore a receipt from a thick pad, laid it with the original form and pressed down a stamp with a toothed border.

A brass tap stood behind the window. A man filling a metal kettle held its mouth close without letting it touch. The drinking-water pipe, marked in pale blue, came from the reservoir at the shore treatment works. The valve cabinet for boiler water stood on the other side, its door locked. A thick pipe near the lake drew process water through settling and filtration equipment before delivering it to valves marked with black rings. The keys to the two cabinets hung on different hooks. One worker opened a cabinet to inspect it while another stayed by the coupling, watching the hose from the ship.

“Give it to the engine-room watch when you’re back,” the clerk said. “The relief watch knows this paper too.”

Fenn put the papers away and walked back a little more slowly.

The streets began just behind the docks.

Most buildings along the street had arcades below. The columns were not large, but their feet had been solidly built, worn shiny by carts and wet ropes. The shops had tall, narrow windows divided into small panes. Grey-green roofs rose up the slope in orderly stages, their corners neatly turned, a narrow pale line beneath the eaves. A new bank, a pharmacy, a cobbler and even a bathhouse shedding its plaster all kept much the same mouldings and window proportions.

The great trading house had a taller doorway and finer ironwork above it. Its warehouses ran from the street all the way to the water. The same family name was stamped on every crate. Someone polished the brass door handle each morning; round the other side, dozens of men were loading flour bearing that name onto carts. The manager stood beneath the arcade, checking the bills one by one. When a shipowner he knew arrived, he moved the chair beside him and invited him to sit.

At the street corner a few large trees were just coming into leaf. Low stone rims enclosed the raised soil around their roots, with tough grass planted inside. An old tea seller had set up beneath them. Her kettle stood on a little coal stove, and she had wiped the table clean with a damp cloth. The leaves were not yet thick, so she had added a reed mat between two poles to shield her customers from the slanting sun.

Farther up the slope, the light suddenly thinned.

The bone cliff blocked the southern sky. The heights Fenn had been able to see a moment ago were cut in half by the street roofs. Turning into a short lane, he found a slanting overhang of bone blocking still more. The shops beneath it had their windows open, but lamps were burning inside. A gas flame behind a frosted globe lit only a small patch of blue cloth under a tailor’s hands. At the back of the shop an apprentice laid his needle on white paper to find it, picked it up and went on finishing a cuff.

Someone high above was adjusting a daylighting mirror.

On a narrow maintenance ledge a worker turned a handwheel slowly. Gears engaged beneath the mirror frame with a fine, even sound. A bright reflection travelled along a wall, passed a window frame and lingered at the mouth of the lane. A child stretched his hand into it and spread his fingers to watch their shadow on the ground. His mother, wringing out a sheet, told him to move aside and shifted her drying rack into the light.

Fenn looked up out of the reflected sunlight. The lip of the cliff was bright, its white surface streaked with thin water marks that disappeared behind the roofs. The ridge hid the streets above. From here he could see only a nearby flight of stone steps clinging to the slope and the maintenance labels nailed along a wall. Their numbers had been repainted; the old paint still showed around the edges.

At the corner hung a Throughbone Line stop sign, with a route map under glass. Lamplight Arcade appeared near the bottom. A red line ran into the valley, turned several times at Bridge Market and climbed towards Sunterrace. Those places lay beyond the ridge. Two stops took up only a short piece of the map, but quite a few minutes on the timetable beside it. Some connections had a separate little symbol for a cable car.

An advertisement for a Sunterrace hotel was pasted beside the sign. Its terrace was laid with square tiles. Wooden chairs stood behind an iron railing, and a corner of tablecloth had faded white in the sun. A flowerpot held down one edge; leaf shadows fell across a porcelain cup. Fenn looked at the tablecloth first. Only then did he notice the room price printed below.

Sertin City spread along an exposed section on the front of the left seventh rib. Its lower reaches met the lakeshore; the upper neighbourhoods crossed the broad ridge of bone and extended onto the mineralised terraces joined to it. The cliff rose behind the harbour, then eased into gentler slopes covered in patches of soil, roads and houses. Farther away, the rest of the bone remained buried in hills and geological layers. Coming by train the day before, Fenn had passed orchards, fields and other station towns. Through the window he had often been unable to tell which rises had bone beneath them.

Beyond a row of warehouses, a locomotive released steam at the low lakeside station. Trains connecting with the Axial Trunk Railway took the branch back to Shumen; Fenn had changed there yesterday. The Throughbone Line interchange platforms were nearby. Two short tracks entered the train shed, where a steam rack-rail car had just closed its doors. The conductor held the upright by the doorway and signalled a late passenger to slow down. Only when the man was steady did he lift the step.

As the wheels began to move, the cogwheel engaged the rack between the rails. A fine, close-spaced clatter followed the car out of the shed. It climbed the low shore slope and rounded the warehouses, its windows disappearing one after another until only a little steam remained against the bone wall. Fenn looked at the map again. Beyond that, the line still had to enter the valley, pass through short tunnels and several hillside stops before reaching Bridge Market. The through-bone section carried a cable-haulage symbol. Its winch house stood outside. He had not ridden those stretches yet.

Fenn had thought he would remember every shop he passed on the way back. After studying the stop sign and those windows, he nearly turned into the wrong arcade.

A queue stood outside the postal agency. A newspaper boy crouched on the steps, biting through the string around a bundle. He pulled out a sheet and held it farther away than his own arm. Posters on the wall announced the spring flower market, lake rowing races and a new production of A Visitor from the Sea of Mists. On the theatre poster something with many eyes was raising its head from the sea. The eyes had been arranged very evenly. So had the ticket prices beneath them.

Fenn looked at it a little longer.

His mother had warned him too: once he was away from home, he must not stray too far from the bones. She had been sewing a pocket into his clothes when she said it. The needle paused against the cloth before she went on.

Some ships went to the river mouth; others travelled along the coast. The papers printed accounts by people who had gone far away, and shops sold spices said to come from other bones. All those places were distant in Fenn’s mind. For the moment, what he could explain clearly was the cost of each stage from his town to here, and where he had needed to change trains.

A hurried, uneven engine noise suddenly broke out in the street.

A diesel lorry came slowly out of the freight yard. It was square-bodied, with a high bonnet, and its thick tyres made a heavy noise on the paving. There was no horse and no visible boiler, though the load in the back was substantial enough. The driver held the wheel in both hands. Beside him sat a man with a tool bag. At the corner the man leaned half his body out to look at the road before signalling him to turn.

The old tea seller glanced up and turned the kettle’s spout towards the stove.

“That one’s still going,” said the man from the repair shop next door.

“Had a part changed only last week.”

“Which part?”

The other man did not hear him. The lorry had gone by. Several porters waited until it had passed, then hauled their carts across the street. The horses started forward again, a brass bell beneath a shaft ringing with their steps.

The receipt rested stiffly in Fenn’s pocket, one hard corner against his chest. Remembering what Haller had said, he quickened his pace.

Near the pier he saw a few people gathered outside the harbour workshop.

A panel stood tilted on a support, thick-framed and sealed with glass. Beneath the glass were orderly rows of purple-brown wafers, joined by metal wires so fine he could only make them out up close. The black seal around the edge was still fresh. Two insulated wires led from the bottom into a wooden box. Its lid was open. Inside stood a storage battery, with a needle meter beside it.

Fenn recognised the words on the sign: SOLAR-CELL TRIAL.

He had read the introduction at the mechanics school. The printed illustration was so dark that the wafers, glass and wires all blurred together. This panel was heavier than he had imagined, and more ordinary. A corner of its frame still had packing paper stuck to it.

A technician in a canvas apron was cleaning the glass.

“It’s for the trial light at the gorge mouth,” he told the people beside him. “Charge it in the day, light one small lamp at night. We still have to test it through a run of cloudy days. Spare batteries have to be sent up too.”

Someone asked whether it could run a crane. The technician pointed towards the water, where a crane had just lifted a full grab of coal. Its heavy turning could be heard even here.

“Let this one feed that little lamp first.”

A few people laughed. Fenn did not. He was watching the needle. The technician wiped one corner of the glass and moved the cloth away. The needle shifted slightly.

The technician noticed his cap ribbon.

“From the Elena?”

“Yes. New to the engine room.”

“Then take a message for me. The last batch of lamp holders they took out can come over this afternoon. Tell them not to pick mealtime again.”

Fenn agreed and took one more look at the panel before he left.

Sunlight lay across the glass. The needle was small. He had to come close to see it move.

* * *

When he returned aboard, the shadows on the quay had shortened.

Haller took the receipt, found the loading time and glanced at his own watch. He listened to the message about the lamp holders, then told Fenn to find the chief engineer and repeat it to him.

The captain stood beside the bridge.

This time Fenn set his feet properly before saluting. The captain wore a well-fitting dark-blue service coat, the cloth finer than the sailors’ uniforms, with silver braid running all the way around the cuffs. His stiff cap peak shaded a small patch above his eyebrows. A flat leather holster hung at his side. The grip of a Bridgeguard pistol showed beside its flap, one corner of the dark grip plate worn very smooth.

The captain returned the salute and asked whether he had been seasick.

“No, sir.”

“Learn your way about the lake first. Go and find the chief.”

He looked back down at the navigation notice in his hand. A clip held its corner against the wind. An officer beside him pointed to a line, and the captain made a short pencil mark in the margin.

Fenn stepped away. Only as he went down the ladder did he realise that this time he had not pulled at his cuff.

The chief engineer was at a narrow desk on the upper level of the engine room.

Several logbooks lay on the desk, their page edges darkened by oil and fingers, the handwriting inside still neat. On the wall hung a simple waterway chart and a repair board. Lamp holders, pump parts and spare packing were listed there, with dates filled in against each item. When Fenn gave him the harbour workshop’s message, the chief put a finger on the lamp-holder entry.

“Someone’s taking them over this afternoon. What’s your name?”

Fenn told him.

“Tended pumps in the country?”

“For someone else. I’ve read the school’s elementary engine-room textbook.”

The chief pushed his spectacles farther up his nose and led him inside.

Electric lights burned in the engine room even in the daytime.

Milky glass shades sat in protective metal cages, spaced along the bulkheads and beneath the beams. The edges of iron ladders, the corners of narrow platforms, even a passage behind the machinery that a man could only enter sideways, all had enough light to be made out. Cables were clipped to wooden battens. The switch-box covers were fastened shut, and spare oil lamps in glass housings hung nearby.

Fenn smelled hot oil and coal. Farther down, the heat grew solid around him, working its way inside his collar. Even the rail beneath his hand held a little warmth.

The main engine was stopped.

It occupied much of the centre of the compartment. Polished piston rods descended from above to cranks and connecting rods below. Large nuts stood exposed, their corners wiped clean. Recent inspection marks showed on the iron near the bearings. The pumps Fenn had known also had rods, shafts and reciprocating motion. Here those familiar relationships had been joined together, layer upon layer, until he had to tilt his head back to see the upper parts.

A small auxiliary engine was running beside it, its rapid beat close and compact. The generator it drove sat behind a guard, giving off a steady hum. Other pumps were working. Water moved from somewhere he could not hear to somewhere else he could not hear, while the slender needles on the pressure gauges kept their separate stations.

When the stokehold door opened, heat poured out. The fire was held behind iron doors; only an orange-red line escaped through a crack. The stoker wore an open-necked work coat with the sleeves rolled up. Coal dust marked the side of his neck. He rested his shovel securely, nodded to the chief and turned to check another gauge.

The chief stopped Fenn at the edge of the platform.

“Learn these first. Read the gauges, note the time, listen. When the watch changes, look at everything the last watch has left you.”

He did not explain the machinery from the beginning. He simply asked Fenn a few questions. Fenn could answer about the pumps and water supply, but hesitated over another pipe. The chief made him read its label, then took him a little farther along the platform.

Each part had its own place. Removed nuts went into a little box; a detached cover rested on a timber-lined rack. A sailor was cleaning parts at a bench, clean and used cloths kept on opposite sides. He shifted over a little. The chief told Fenn to sit there and spread out an old drawing.

Before long, Fenn undid a button at his collar.

He recognised the lines on the drawing, but had to go round the machinery to find all the actual parts. Some were hidden behind others, and he had to bend to see them. Some sounds came up from under his feet; the first time, he judged one wrongly. The sailor beside him moved his pencil point half an inch. Fenn followed it with his eyes and at last found the thin pipe.

He asked whether he might copy the drawing. The chief said yes: enough for what he needed now, and he could carry on in the evening.

That pleased Fenn. In town he had to borrow other people’s books, and could not take them away. When the day came to return one, there were always two or three pages he had not quite understood.

Near eleven, Haller came to find him.

“Up you go. Learn your station for noon.”

Fenn folded his paper and put it back in his pocket. At the ladder he looked back at the lamps. The little auxiliary engine was still beating steadily, and the light stayed steady throughout the compartment.

Only when the deck wind touched him did he realise how wet his back was with sweat.

The cover had been taken off the bow gun.

The canvas had been folded into a thick square, tied and moved aside. The gun shield made a grey curve, rivets lined along its edge. Corners and places often touched by hands were a little darker. The barrel projected through it, heavy and straight. Its paint was not as handsome as in the magazine, but it covered the little knocks. Oil shone at a few metal joints.

The gun captain crouched nearby, looking over a record. The others each had work to do; no one stood idly around the muzzle. Fenn followed Haller to a marked line and stopped. The bow pointed towards open water. Nearby work had been moved clear. A duty sailor below watched the small boats and kept them out of that stretch.

Passing the stern, he saw the Gat gun again.

A corner of its cover had been lifted. The barrels made a tight ring; the mounting joined a base fixed to the deck. A piece of old cloth wrapped the crank at the side so that it would not bruise anyone in the narrow space. A sailor was wiping the mounting, drawing the dirt slowly down the steel. His tools lay on a cloth beside him, each small piece in its own place.

Fenn watched for a moment, then caught up with Haller.

The lines for the afternoon patrol had been recoiled, the hoses rolled up, and buckets and cleaning gear returned to their appointed places. Someone was checking the oars in a boat. Someone else was replacing a lamp shade with a cracked rim at the bow. The loading noises beside the ship were growing fewer; ashore, work was still busy.

Haller gave him half a rag.

“Stern. Give the name a wipe. Don’t splash water into the gaps.”

Fenn crouched and first took off a thin layer of dust.

The brass letters slowly brightened beneath his hand. Some edges at their feet had worn round; the fine recessed grooves held a darker colour. Below them, the builder’s plate was more than ten years old, recording the yard and inspection number. A rivet at its far edge had been replaced and still looked new.

The white ship he had seen in yesterday’s magazine was now many things his hands could touch: a length of flaking rail, a locker lid that would not quite shut, socks drying out of the wind, a wooden deck dusted with coal again after it had been wiped. The people aboard took turns tending these things, and the Elena could go out as usual.

A gust came round the funnel and deposited coal dust on the spot he had just cleaned.

Haller saw it and nudged a brush towards his feet.

“Is this mine too?” Fenn asked.

“The wind doesn’t take orders from me.”

Fenn picked up the brush.

Half a deck away, two sailors were negotiating Sunday’s watch. One wanted to see his daughter in the rowing races. The other wanted him to take his next early watch as well.

“She doesn’t race every day.”

“And I don’t get out of bed for you every day.”

They argued quietly for a little while, fetched the roster and checked the two names again. Haller passed, looked at their changes and told them to have the officer of the watch sign them. Then he went on.

By the time Fenn finished the letters, his fingers ached a little. He found a reasonably clean part of the rag and went round the edges once more.

Lake water trembled in the brass, together with the broken roofs and windows on its surface.

Eleven fifty-seven.

The telephone below the bridge rang. The officer of the watch lifted the receiver and signalled towards the deck. Haller checked the clock, came over and moved the bucket beside Fenn out of the way.

“First time this close?”

“I heard it from the lodgings yesterday.”

“Then get inside the doorway. Put your earplugs in properly.”

Fenn stowed his things as told and stepped back to the assigned place. A cook was coming past with a basin of peeled potatoes and told him not to block the way. He hurried another step aside. Water shifted gently in the basin, turning a few potatoes over.

Ashore, the watchmakers had already raised their watches.

Behind them, cranes were still turning and carts still moving. A worker with a large basket on his back set it on the steps and sat down to rest. The ferry came alongside; a line was thrown ashore, and the waiting passengers pressed towards the bow. The little girl with the flowerpot was finally lifted onto the stone steps.

The mirror adjusted that morning sent a small patch of sunlight into the deep lane. The tailor moved his blue cloth into it and narrowed his eyes at the last stretch of stitching.

A brief order came from the bridge.

The gun fired.

The shock travelled through the iron wall and struck his shoulder. Fenn knew it was coming, but his neck still drew down.

The report crossed the lake, swept over the docks and entered the streets along the shore. It struck echoes from the nearby white slope of bone. A few waterbirds lifted low over the water. On Lakeside Street the cat jumped from the sill, and the old woman turned to lift the lid of her soup pot.

The watchmakers looked down and moved their watch hands. The man resting on the steps unwrapped his lunch. The baker’s assistant brought the first basket of midday bread outside and called the price across the street. At a farther corner, a small bell took up the time, its clear notes following for a little way.

The vibration left Fenn’s shoulder quickly. His palms were still tight.

Haller patted his arm.

“Go and eat.”

Beside the mess-room door stood an enamel basin. Warm water flowed from a small tap; a fine chain held the soap dish beside it. Fenn washed his hands. The water carried black dust from between his fingers, though a little stayed around his nails. He wiped them carefully before taking a bowl.

Steam rose from a great pot of stew: beans, onions and meat cut into small pieces. Potatoes were heaped in another basin, not too thoroughly peeled. In a bowl they quickly took on a coating of gravy. Thick slices of bread lay on wooden trays for people to take as they passed. The tables were long and fixed to the floor, their corners rubbed round by many sleeves and elbows.

The men eating first kept portions for those still on watch. The cook filled a few covered meal tins and had them taken to the engine room. When Fenn sat down, the sailor who had been sewing his button that morning was wiping gravy from the edge of his bowl with bread. He asked whether Fenn had found his way about the ship yet.

“More or less.”

“‘More or less’ means not yet.”

The man smiled and moved the kettle out from between them.

Fenn first drank some hot broth. He had spent all morning learning names and places; only now did he taste the sweetness of the softened onion. At the next table they were talking about a new quay. One man said the wider berth meant the tug no longer had to squeeze past the old piles. Another said they had raised the mooring fees, and sooner or later the owners would make the money back somewhere else.

Haller came to eat and handed the signed roster back to the two sailors. The one who wanted to see his daughter race folded it, put it in his breast pocket and began to eat much faster.

“She won’t go anywhere,” his friend said. “It’s still not till Sunday.”

“I need to find out where they’re launching from first.”

Fenn bent over his bread and broke it open. The crust was rather hard, but the inside was soft. He dipped it in the broth and picked up even the few crumbs that had fallen on the table.

After the meal, Haller took him to the clerk to finish signing the register.

The clerk turned the book round and put a finger beside an empty line. Fenn wrote his name, then his home town and reporting date. He wrote very neatly. One stroke crowded the edge of a box; he paused and carefully finished it.

“Use this address when anyone sends you things.” The clerk gave him a slip of paper. “Ship’s name, unit, recipient. The mailbag goes ashore before we sail. If you’ve a letter, be quick.”

Fenn took the slip.

His letter home was still inside the book of engine diagrams he had brought. The night before, at the temporary lodgings, he had written only the salutation. Then he heard someone hauling freight outside and went to help shift a cart blocking the door. By the time he came back he was sleepy. He had slipped the writing paper into his book and packed it with his spare clothes.

Now he sat at the little table in the mess deck and unfolded it again.

People were moving above his head. Several pairs of soles crossed the deck overhead. A telephone rang once and was quickly answered. The smell of stew lingered in the air. Work clothes hanging nearby were slowly drying, a corner of the cloth stirring in the air from a ventilator.

Suddenly there was a great deal Fenn wanted to tell his mother.

He wanted to write about the bone slope with its trees and bridges, large enough to hide a train; the boats lying side by side on the lake; the lorry without a horse; and the glass panel that made a needle move when sunlight fell on it. He wanted to say that the boatswain was less imposing than the men in the magazine and wore his shoes thin at the edges; that the captain himself had asked whether he was seasick; that the chief engineer had told him to stay in the evening and copy drawings.

All these things crowded beneath his pen, and for a moment made it harder to begin.

Someone outside was calling for the mailbag.

He hurried out a few lines, folded the paper and put it in an envelope, copying the ship’s name and address from the little slip. The ink was not quite dry. He held the letter beneath the ventilator for a moment before handing it to the clerk.

The mailbag went ashore before they sailed.

The afternoon wind came across the lake, rattling the signal flags against their lines. The Elena’s deck was clear. Men had taken their places by the moorings, and the boatswain made one last walk along the passage. Fenn followed the sailor supervising him back to the engine-room platform. He stood where he had learned to stand that morning, recording paper at hand and the unfinished copy of the drawing in his pocket.

The machinery’s sound began to change.

The heavy connecting rods began to move. Steel that had been still passed from light to shadow; brightness slid down the polished piston rods and rose again. The main shaft turned beneath his feet, carrying its sound through the whole hull. Even the rail in his hand felt different from the morning. Fenn watched the supervising sailor make an entry. Only after the man nodded did he write down his own reading.

Water stirred astern. White foam drew back along the hull.

The men taking in the lines ashore raised their hands. Ferry passengers watched from behind the rail, and a few children waved at the warship. An empty wooden crate in the water beside the stone steps turned half a circle, then a small wave pushed it gently back.

The Elena moved slowly away from the pier.

Sunlight travelled with her along the hull, passing over each place that had been wiped or patched. Grey-green roofs and the high white slope of bone receded beyond the bridge windows. The marks towards the east shore gradually formed a different line.

On the quay the postman put the warship’s mailbag on his cart, tied it down and bent to push over a stretch of stone paving. The cart bell rang twice beneath the arcade. He had to catch the next local train to Central Axis Station.

Fenn’s letter lay among many others. The paper was thin.

He told his mother that he had reported for duty, that his clothes fitted, that the food suited him, and that the chief engineer had let him copy drawings. At the end he added one more thing he thought they ought to know at home.

The ship has electric lights. They keep them on in the daytime, too.
