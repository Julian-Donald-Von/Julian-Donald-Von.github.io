Worldbook · An introduction

A civilisation among the giant bones

One enormous skeleton, twenty-four member states, and a history of lives shared.

This is an English overview. The complete public gazetteers are currently available in Chinese. Unchecked full-length machine translations have not been published.

Land, bone and ordinary ground

The giant lies on its back without a wholesale rotation of the body. Much of the skeleton is buried, while weathering, erosion and geological change have left discontinuous exposures. People live on bone terraces, beside cliffs, in accessible shallow cavities and on ordinary soil-covered land. Not every bone is exposed, and not every opening leads to a vast habitable interior.

The rear of the skull and the deeper skull base are largely buried. Accessible spaces near the face and eye sockets admit daylight into parts of the royal capital's university districts. On the arms and legs, weathered surfaces support broad terraces and local lakes, while infilled valleys carry farms and roads.

The seventh rib's designated bony exposure has a footprint of about 429 square kilometres and reaches roughly 900 metres above the surrounding surface. The adjoining mineralised connecting terrace covers about 1,782 square kilometres and reaches higher exposed levels of about 1,400–1,700 metres. Supported by underlying strata and bone, this terrace is not a roof suspended over an entire lake basin. Shade follows actual slopes, cliffs, recessed faces and shallow cavities.

A concord of different governments

The Concord of the Giant-Bone States brings together twenty-four autonomous members. Their political boundaries do not match the anatomical divisions of the skeleton. A state may occupy parts of several bones and adjoining ordinary ground, while a single rib region may contain territories of more than one government.

Shared transport standards, postal connections, surveying and the settlement of disputes coexist with local laws, budgets, schools and police. Royal, civic, mercantile, communal and institutional governments all belong to the same civilisation. The royal court and the common institutions exercise different powers; sharing a political centre does not make them one government.

The Central High Plateau Common District and the six Unadministered and Disputed Lands are recorded separately. Neither is an additional member state.

Steam, craft and unequal access to light

Steam remains the principal source of mechanical power. Steam railways, rack railways, boats, cable haulage and animal transport connect settlements at different levels. Early internal-combustion vehicles are scarce. Hand-cranked telephones, telegraph offices, workshops and local generating plants form selective networks rather than a universally connected modern society.

Electricity exists, but access depends on district, institution, contract and maintenance. Factories and railways may have dedicated supplies while nearby households rely on fuel lamps and daylight. Experimental photovoltaic devices coexist with poor domestic provision. Redirecting sunlight through mirrors redistributes a limited supply of light; it does not create energy or make every deep cavity suitable for crops.

Weapons in the baseline period include manually cycled magazine rifles, self-loading pistols, revolvers, lever-action rifles, pump-action shotguns, hand-cranked Gat guns and artillery. A self-loading pistol does not imply that every army has automatic machine guns. Civilian restrictions and local military patterns differ in their institutions and implementation.

Food, work and the production of wealth

Ordinary fields, forests, mines and fisheries supply the civilisation. Bone material is a regulated building and chemical feedstock, not a source of every metal or fuel. Railways and waterways carry coal, timber, grain and manufactured goods between members.

Climate, soils, altitude, water, labour and transport shape production. Regions may share a crop or animal while supplying different grades and markets. Everyday grain, dairy produce and beer coexist with fine fibres, selected wines and expensive protected horticulture. Some rare goods depend on difficult distant trade. Prestige does not guarantee that a crop is abundant or that every household can afford it.

Courts, banks, merchants, landlords, factories and public institutions draw wealth from property, credit, contracts, trade and labour. Their consumption supports real supply chains and employment, while unequal wages, rents, services and political rights create everyday tensions.

Music, books and public life

Music travels through performance, touring companies, schools, printed scores and lyric sheets. Guitars, bowed strings, piano, organ, accordion, harmonica, brass, woodwinds and percussion support styles ranging from ceremonial and chamber music to popular songs, folk traditions and rough, strongly rhythmic ensembles. Acoustic instruments retain their physical limits.

Mechanical phonographs and recording workshops exist in limited numbers. They do not require universal household electricity, but cost, wear, volume and recording quality restrict their use. Theatre, literature, visual crafts, stage illusion and local forms of comic storytelling have their own performers, institutions and audiences.

Shared holidays coexist with civic anniversaries and local seasonal customs. Architecture follows a coherent language within each state, while landscape, planting and access to green space vary between places and social groups.

Travel and common belief

Most residents believe that lasting human settlement depends on the giant bones. Stories about distant lands shape trade restrictions, travel habits, scholarship and popular literature. Public knowledge, local experience and official policy do not always coincide.

This edition presents the world as its inhabitants encounter it. Passages revealing later plot, private author plans or hidden information have been omitted from the public collection.
